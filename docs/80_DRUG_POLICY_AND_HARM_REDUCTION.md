# CivicOS — Drug Policy and Harm Reduction (Companion 80)

This companion specifies how CivicOS approaches drug policy — a profoundly contested domain where sovereign discretion varies widely. It complements Companion 38 §9 (mental health), Companion 70 §9 (decriminalization), Companion 22 §5.4 (vulnerability), and Companion 24 §6 (privacy) by going deep on the operational and ethical design of platform support for drug policy across the spectrum from prohibition to decriminalization to regulation.

The thesis: **drug policy is sovereign choice with profound consequences for citizens — health, dignity, civil liberties, family, community**. CivicOS does not advocate any particular drug policy framework but supports the structural commitments that protect citizen rights regardless of the framework chosen: health-based responses to use; harm reduction where chosen; due process in any criminal enforcement; anti-discrimination in policing and treatment; protection of vulnerable users; treatment access; data privacy in health-based responses; community involvement; reparative posture for past punitive harms where applicable.

The discipline: sovereign discretion respected; civil liberties strict in any enforcement; harm reduction supported where chosen; treatment accessible; data sealed; anti-criminalization of harm reduction services; anti-discrimination in policing; reparative posture for past disproportionate criminalization where applicable; cross-sovereign cooperation respects sovereignty.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Sovereign discretion respected.** Drug policy varies; CivicOS supports the chosen framework with structural rights safeguards.
2. **Civil liberties strict.** In any enforcement, due process and anti-discrimination apply.
3. **Health-based response supported.** Where sovereign chooses, treatment infrastructure available.
4. **Harm reduction supported.** Where chosen; anti-criminalization of services.
5. **Treatment access universal.** Where treatment is the framework.
6. **Data privacy strict.** Health and treatment data sealed.
7. **Anti-discrimination in policing.** Disparate impact monitored and remediated.
8. **Vulnerable users protected.** Anti-stigma; trauma-informed.
9. **Community involvement.** Affected communities have voice.
10. **Reparative posture.** For past disproportionate harm where applicable.

---

## 2. Sovereign frameworks

| Framework | Description | CivicOS support |
|---|---|---|
| **Strict prohibition** | Criminalization of all use, possession, sale | Per Companion 70 due process safeguards |
| **Limited prohibition** | Some substances criminalized; others regulated | Differentiated support |
| **Decriminalization** | Use/possession not criminal; sale may remain | Health-based response infrastructure |
| **Regulated legalization** | Sale and use legal under regulation | Regulatory framework support |
| **Medical exception** | Medical use legal; recreational varies | Per medical regulatory framework |
| **Indigenous traditional use** | Customary use of certain substances respected | Per Companion 36 customary practice |

CivicOS operates across; sovereigns choose.

## 3. Health-based responses

### 3.1 The principle

Where sovereign chooses health-based response to drug use, CivicOS supports treatment, harm reduction, and recovery.

### 3.2 Mechanisms

- Treatment program access through CivicHealth.
- Methadone/buprenorphine programs where applicable.
- Naloxone distribution where applicable.
- Mental health support per Companion 38 §9.
- Community-based treatment.
- Recovery support communities.
- Continuous care.

### 3.3 Discipline

- Treatment voluntary unless court-ordered with strict due process.
- Trauma-informed.
- Cultural sensitivity.
- Anti-discrimination in treatment access.
- Confidentiality strict (treatment data sealed).

### 3.4 Forbidden

- Coerced treatment absent due process.
- Use of treatment data for non-clinical purposes.
- Discrimination in treatment access.
- Surveillance through treatment infrastructure.

## 4. Harm reduction

### 4.1 The principle

Where sovereign chooses, harm reduction services reduce death and disease without requiring abstinence.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- Needle/syringe exchange programs.
- Drug testing services.
- Supervised consumption sites where chosen.
- Naloxone distribution.
- Education and outreach.
- Connection to treatment for those seeking it.

### 4.3 Discipline

- Anti-criminalization of harm reduction services and workers.
- Anti-criminalization of users accessing services.
- Confidentiality strict.
- Cultural sensitivity.

### 4.4 Forbidden

- Criminalization of harm reduction services.
- Use of harm reduction service data for prosecution.
- Discrimination in harm reduction access.
- Surveillance through harm reduction infrastructure.

## 5. Criminal enforcement (where applicable)

### 5.1 The principle

Where criminal frameworks remain, civil liberties safeguards strict.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

Per Companion 70:
- Due process strict.
- Anti-discriminatory enforcement.
- Anti-mass-incarceration.
- Diversion to treatment where appropriate.
- Anti-algorithmic-determinism in drug cases.

### 5.3 Discipline

- Disparate impact monitored.
- Anti-discrimination remediated.
- Anti-pretextual stops.
- Right to counsel.

### 5.4 Forbidden

- Algorithmic predictive enforcement.
- Discriminatory enforcement based on race, religion, region.
- Use of platform for political prosecution disguised as drug enforcement.
- Mandatory minimums removing judicial discretion (where constitutionally appropriate).

## 7. Indigenous traditional use

Per Companion 36:

### 7.1 The principle

Some indigenous communities have traditional use of certain substances (peyote, ayahuasca, coca, kava, etc.). Where customarily lawful, respected.

### 7.2 Mechanisms

- Recognition of customary use within community contexts.
- Cultural and religious practice protections.
- Anti-criminalization of customary practice within community.
- Anti-appropriation of customary medicines for commercial use without benefit sharing.

### 7.3 Discipline

- Communities lead.
- Cultural and religious sensitivity.
- Anti-imposition of dominant cultural frameworks.

### 7.4 Forbidden

- Criminalization of customary indigenous substance use.
- Commercial appropriation of indigenous medicines.
- Surveillance of customary practice.

## 8. Vulnerable populations

### 8.1 People who use drugs

- Anti-stigma.
- Health-based services accessible.
- Anti-discrimination in service access.
- Harm reduction available.
- Treatment available.

### 8.2 Pregnant users

- Health-based response (treatment, not punishment).
- Confidentiality strict.
- Family preservation prioritized where consistent with safety.
- Anti-mandatory-reporting that deters care-seeking.

### 8.3 Youth users

- Treatment programs age-appropriate.
- Education emphasized.
- Anti-criminalization for youth where decriminalization is policy.
- Family engagement per youth and family needs.

### 8.4 Forbidden

- Algorithmic targeting of vulnerable users.
- Punishment of pregnant users seeking care.
- Coerced family separation based on drug use absent strict due process.
- Surveillance of vulnerable users through health services.

## 9. Community involvement

### 9.1 The principle

Affected communities have voice in drug policy and program design.

### 9.2 Mechanisms

- Citizens' Assembly review of drug policy changes.
- Affected community representation in program design.
- Civil society organizations standing.
- Peer involvement in services (people with lived experience).

### 9.3 Discipline

- Anti-cooptation.
- Plurality of voices.
- Cultural and contextual sensitivity.

### 9.4 Forbidden

- Top-down imposition of programs without community input.
- Discrimination against affected community voices.

## 10. Reparative posture

### 10.1 The principle

In many sovereigns, drug enforcement has been disproportionately harmful to specific communities (often racial/ethnic minorities, indigenous communities, the poor). Where past harms occurred, reparative posture.

### 10.2 Mechanisms

- Expungement of past convictions where decriminalization or regulation is current policy.
- Restitution for affected individuals and communities.
- Equity in legal market access.
- Investment in disproportionately harmed communities.
- Acknowledgment of past harm.

### 10.3 Discipline

- Honest historical reckoning.
- Affected community leadership in reparative measures.
- Long-term commitment.

### 10.4 Forbidden

- Hidden continuation of past punitive patterns.
- Use of new framework for new disproportionate enforcement.

## 12. Data privacy in drug policy

### 12.1 The principle

Drug-related data — treatment, harm reduction service use, regulatory compliance — is among the most sensitive.

### 12.2 Mechanisms

- Sealed compartments for treatment data (per Companion 38 §4).
- Confidentiality strict in harm reduction services.
- Anti-data-sharing with non-clinical parties.
- Anti-cross-purpose use.
- Per-RP UID in any platform interactions.

### 12.3 Forbidden

- Use of treatment or harm reduction data for criminal prosecution.
- Use of regulatory data for adverse decisions in unrelated contexts (employment, housing, child custody, etc., absent specific legal basis).
- Cross-border sharing of drug-related data without consent.

## 15. The drug policy north star

Drug policy is profoundly contested; sovereign discretion varies widely. CivicOS does not advocate any particular framework but supports structural rights safeguards in any framework: due process strict in enforcement, health-based response where chosen, harm reduction supported where chosen, treatment accessible, data sealed, anti-discrimination, vulnerable user protection, community involvement, reparative posture for past harms where applicable.

When CivicOS becomes a tool of disproportionate drug enforcement, surveillance through treatment, criminalization of harm reduction, commercial exploitation of users, indigenous appropriation, or political prosecution disguised as drug policy — it has failed. Capability without rights safeguards in drug policy is not progress; it is the institutionalization of harm to people who use drugs.

When the platform supports drug policy with structural rights safeguards — through chosen sovereign framework, civil liberties protection, treatment access, harm reduction support, anti-discrimination, vulnerable user protection, community involvement, reparative posture — it earns the right to be infrastructure for societies addressing drug-related challenges with dignity.

The discipline is daily. The civil liberties are strict. The data confidentiality is real. The anti-discrimination is structural. The community involvement is honored.

Drug policy is sovereign choice; how citizens are treated under that choice is rights commitment. The platform serves the rights commitment regardless of the policy framework. Anything less abandons people who use drugs to disproportionate harm dressed as policy.
