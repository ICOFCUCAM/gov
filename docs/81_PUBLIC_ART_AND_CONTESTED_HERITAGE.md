# CivicOS — Public Art, Monuments, and Contested Heritage (Companion 81)

This companion specifies how CivicOS approaches public art, monuments, statues, plaques, public space naming, and contested heritage. It complements Companion 53 (arts, culture, civic life), Companion 35 (knowledge commons), Companion 36 (indigenous communities), Companion 49 (peacebuilding), and Companion 71 (truth and reconciliation) by going deep on the operational and ethical design of platform support for public commemoration that is itself often contested.

The thesis: **public art and monuments shape civic identity and collective memory; they are contested precisely because they matter**. Many sovereigns face debates about colonial monuments, civil war commemorations, slavery memorials, dictator-era statues, indigenous representation, gender representation in public art, and broader questions of who decides what is publicly commemorated and how. CivicOS does not impose answers but supports deliberative processes — Citizens' Assemblies, indigenous community sovereignty, civil society engagement, plurality of voices, multi-perspective preservation — for the work communities must do themselves.

The discipline: public art commissioning is participatory, transparent, and plural; contested monuments addressed through deliberative process; indigenous community sovereignty over depictions affecting them; multi-perspective preservation of difficult history; anti-erasure balanced with anti-celebration of harm; long-horizon stewardship; civil society and academic engagement; community-led for community contexts.

## 2. Public art commissioning

### 2.1 The principle

Public art is commissioned through participatory, transparent, plural processes.

### 2.2 Mechanisms

- Public art commissions through arts authority (per Companion 53 §2.1).
- Open competition where appropriate.
- Community involvement in site-specific work.
- Indigenous and minority artist representation (per Companion 25, Companion 36).
- Anti-discrimination in commissioning.
- Funding insulated from political pressure (per Companion 53 §2.1).

### 2.3 Discipline

- Plurality of artists and styles.
- Geographic distribution across sovereign.
- Anti-monopoly in commissioning.
- Community input on site-specific work.

### 2.4 Forbidden

- Political control of public art commissioning.
- Discrimination in artist selection.
- Use of public art for state propaganda.
- Commissioning that violates community sovereignty (e.g., depictions of indigenous communities without FPIC).

## 3. Contested monuments

### 3.1 The pattern

Many sovereigns have monuments commemorating colonial figures, dictators, slaveholders, war atrocities, or other contested subjects. Debates about removal, contextualization, replacement, or preservation are ongoing.

### 3.2 Mechanisms

- Citizens' Assembly review of major contested monuments.
- Civil society and academic engagement.
- Affected community involvement (descendants of victims, indigenous communities, etc.).
- Multiple perspectives in deliberation.
- Long-horizon view (impact on future generations).
- Plurality of approaches: removal, recontextualization, replacement, museum relocation.

### 3.3 Discipline

- Process over destruction.
- Affected community voice central.
- Anti-erasure of historical record (record preserved even if monument removed).
- Cultural and religious sensitivity.

### 3.4 Approaches

- **Removal**: monument removed from public space; preserved in museum or archive with context.
- **Recontextualization**: monument remains but with added interpretive material acknowledging contested nature.
- **Counter-monument**: additional monuments added providing alternative perspectives.
- **Replacement**: monument removed and replaced with alternative.
- **Preservation as-is**: where community deliberation favors keeping unchanged.

The right answer varies by context.

### 3.5 Forbidden

- Vandalism without process.
- Removal that erases historical record.
- Preservation without acknowledgment where harm celebrated.
- Politicization that bypasses community process.

## 4. Indigenous community sovereignty over depictions

Per Companion 36:

### 4.1 The principle

Indigenous communities have sovereignty over public depictions of them.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- FPIC for public art commissioning involving indigenous communities.
- Community veto on depictions community finds harmful.
- Community-stewarded representation.
- Indigenous artists prioritized for indigenous subjects.
- Cultural protocols respected.

### 4.3 Discipline

- Communities lead.
- Anti-appropriation.
- Anti-stereotyping.
- Community-determined acceptability.

### 4.4 Forbidden

- Public depictions of indigenous communities without FPIC.
- Stereotypical or harmful representations.
- Commercial exploitation of indigenous imagery.
- Use of public art to obscure colonial history.

## 5. Public space naming

### 5.1 The pattern

Streets, parks, public buildings, and other public spaces carry names. Naming is itself a form of public commemoration.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

- Naming through participatory processes.
- Citizens' Assembly for major naming.
- Community involvement for local naming.
- Renaming through similar process where contested.
- Indigenous community involvement in indigenous-area naming.
- Multilingual naming where appropriate.

### 5.3 Discipline

- Plurality in honored figures.
- Anti-discrimination in naming.
- Cultural and historical sensitivity.

### 5.4 Forbidden

- Political renaming that bypasses community.
- Discriminatory naming patterns.
- Erasure of community-significant names without consent.

### 6.2 Mechanisms

- Academic engagement.
- Plurality of historical interpretations.

### 6.3 Discipline

- Anti-celebration of harm.

### 6.4 Forbidden

## 7. Cultural and religious sensitivity

### 7.1 The principle

Public art and commemoration affect cultural and religious sensibilities. Sensitivity required.

### 7.2 Mechanisms

- Religious and cultural community consultation.
- Anti-blasphemy considered without overriding free expression.
- Plurality of cultural and religious traditions represented.
- Anti-discrimination in religious and cultural representation.

### 7.3 Discipline

- Anti-imposition of dominant culture.
- Plurality respected.
- Constitutional rights to free expression preserved alongside sensitivity.

### 7.4 Forbidden

- Suppression of cultural or religious expression in art (within constitutional limits).
- State-imposed religious art absent constitutional basis.
- Discrimination in religious or cultural representation.

## 15. The public art and contested heritage north star

Public art and monuments shape civic identity and collective memory. They are contested precisely because they matter. CivicOS does not impose answers but supports deliberative processes — Citizens' Assemblies, indigenous community sovereignty, civil society engagement, plurality of voices, multi-perspective preservation — for the work communities must do themselves.

When CivicOS becomes a tool of state-imposed public art, politicized monument decisions, erasure of marginalized voices, indigenous appropriation, or memorial weaponization — it has failed civic deliberation about who we honor and how we remember. Capability without process discipline in commemoration is not progress; it is the imposition of one narrative on plural societies.

When the platform supports public art and heritage with deliberative processes, plurality, indigenous community sovereignty, multi-perspective preservation, and long-horizon stewardship — it earns the right to be infrastructure for societies negotiating their identities and memories together.

The discipline is daily. The processes are participatory. The plurality is honored. The community sovereignty is real. The long-horizon view is preserved.

Public art is collective work. The platform supports the work; it does not substitute for the deliberation that communities must do themselves about who they are, who they honor, and how they remember.

This is what makes a state platform worthy of plural societies: support for the contested, deliberative work of public commemoration without imposing answers from above.
