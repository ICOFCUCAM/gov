# CivicOS — Indigenous Peoples and Self-Determined Communities (Companion 36)

This companion specifies how CivicOS engages with indigenous peoples, traditional communities, autonomous regions, and other self-determined communities. It complements Companions 22 (dignity), 25 (multilingual & cultural), and 35 (knowledge commons) by going deep on the *political* and *practical* dimensions of platform deployment in contexts where sovereignty itself is plural.

The thesis: **a state platform deployed in a society with indigenous peoples or self-determined communities must serve them on their terms, not assimilate them to a uniform digital frame**. Free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) is the operating principle. Self-determination is honored. Customary governance is respected. Community sovereignty over data, identity, lands, language, and knowledge is structural.

The discipline: deployment in indigenous and self-determined community contexts proceeds with FPIC, on community timelines, with community-controlled engagement, and with explicit community veto over capabilities affecting them. The state does not impose; it offers. Communities choose.

This companion is necessarily written with humility. CivicOS Inc. and any sovereign deploying it must defer to indigenous and community knowledge holders. This document offers principles and patterns; the substance comes from communities themselves.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Self-determination.** Indigenous peoples and self-determined communities determine their relationship with the state and the platform.
2. **Free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC).** No deployment affecting them without their consent.
3. **Community-controlled engagement.** Engagement on community timelines and in community formats.
4. **Cultural sovereignty.** Communities decide what to digitize, how, and on what terms.
5. **Land sovereignty respected.** Customary land tenure honored; cadastral integration on community terms.
6. **Knowledge sovereignty.** Traditional knowledge protected; CARE principles applied.
7. **Linguistic sovereignty.** Indigenous languages stewarded by communities; platform supports.
8. **Customary law accommodated.** Where compatible with constitutional rights; community processes respected.
9. **Plural governance.** Indigenous self-government coexists with state structures.
10. **Reparative posture.** Where past harms have occurred (often through state digitization), remedy and trust-building are prerequisites.

## 2. The basis: international and constitutional frameworks

### 2.1 International frameworks

CivicOS engagement aligns with:

- UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP).
- ILO Convention 169 on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples.
- Convention on Biological Diversity (Article 8(j) on traditional knowledge).
- UN Sustainable Development Goals (with indigenous-specific commitments).
- Regional human rights instruments.

### 2.2 Sovereign constitutional frameworks

Many sovereigns recognize indigenous peoples in their constitutions. CivicOS deployment respects:

- Constitutional recognition of indigenous nations / first nations / traditional communities.
- Treaty rights between sovereigns and indigenous peoples.
- Self-government agreements.
- Land rights and customary tenure.
- Linguistic rights.
- Customary law jurisdiction.

### 2.3 Where these frameworks do not exist

In sovereigns lacking strong indigenous-rights frameworks, CivicOS engagement still applies the principles above — and may help build the legal architecture through dialogue with indigenous peoples and civil society.

## 3. Free, prior, and informed consent

### 3.1 The standard

FPIC is the operating principle for any platform engagement with indigenous peoples or self-determined communities. FPIC is:

- **Free**: without coercion, intimidation, or manipulation.
- **Prior**: before any deployment affecting them.
- **Informed**: with adequate, accessible, intelligible information.
- **Consent**: actual agreement through community-determined processes.

### 3.2 What requires FPIC

- Identity enrollment processes that include the community.
- Land registry digitization affecting community lands.
- Linguistic platform deployment in community languages.
- Health, education, welfare programs in community contexts.
- Data collection that includes community members.
- Any new capability deployment affecting them.

### 3.3 FPIC mechanics

- Community-determined consultation processes (not state-imposed).
- Adequate time and resources for community deliberation.
- Information in accessible languages and formats.
- Independent advice available to community.
- No financial or other inducement that compromises consent.
- Community right to refuse or condition consent.

### 3.4 Forbidden

- Token consultations that lack genuine consent-seeking.
- Time pressure that precludes deliberation.
- Side deals with community members without community process.
- "Consultation fatigue" exploitation.
- Bypassing community processes through individual member outreach.

## 4. Identity in indigenous and community contexts

### 4.1 The principle

Community membership is determined by communities, not by state algorithms.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- Community-controlled membership rolls integrated with CivicID where community chooses.
- Provisional credentials for community members not on rolls (per community process).
- Multiple identity layers (state ID + community membership) when community wishes.
- Recognition of customary names and naming conventions.
- Recognition of customary kinship structures.

### 4.3 Discipline

- Community sovereignty over membership respected.
- State does not unilaterally assign or deny community membership.
- Disenrollment by communities respected (with safeguards against arbitrary deprivation of state benefits).
- Community-state membership disputes resolved through bilateral processes.

### 4.4 Forbidden

- State unilateral assignment of community membership.
- Use of state identity to compel community membership disclosure to commercial parties.
- Denial of state services based on community membership disputes.

## 5. Land sovereignty

### 5.1 The principle

Customary land tenure is real tenure. Cadastral integration honors customary systems.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

- Customary tenure overlays in CivicLand (Volume I §15, Companion 09).
- Community titles supported (group-held).
- Customary boundaries documented per community process.
- Drone/satellite cadastral updates with community participation.
- Community veto on state-driven cadastral changes affecting their lands.

### 5.3 Conflict resolution

- Customary processes respected.
- Joint forums for state-community disputes.
- Independent adjudication where bilateral processes fail.
- Anti-displacement principles operationalized.

### 5.4 Discipline

- No formalization of disputed claims at expense of community holders.
- Anti-corruption discipline applied with extra rigor (land grabs are common in vulnerable contexts).
- Community participation in cadastral processes that affect them.

### 5.5 Forbidden

- Unilateral state cadastral imposition on community lands.
- Use of platform to facilitate dispossession.
- Commercial use of cadastral data on community lands without community consent.

---

## 6. Knowledge sovereignty

### 6.1 The principle

Traditional knowledge belongs to communities. CARE principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) apply.

### 6.2 Mechanisms

- Communities determine what traditional knowledge to share, with whom, on what terms.
- Anti-extraction discipline (Companion 35 §8).
- Benefit-sharing agreements where commercial use occurs.
- Repatriation of misappropriated knowledge.
- Cultural and intellectual property frameworks adapted to traditional knowledge contexts.

### 6.3 Civic Data Trusts

- Community-stewarded data trusts where community wishes.
- Community veto on access to community data.
- Community benefit prioritized over external research interests.

### 6.4 Forbidden

- Platform-enabled appropriation of traditional knowledge.
- Commercial use without community consent.
- Use of traditional knowledge for purposes communities have not authorized.

---

## 7. Linguistic sovereignty

### 7.1 The principle

Indigenous languages are the sovereignty of their speakers. Platform supports; communities steward.

### 7.2 Mechanisms

- Indigenous languages supported in CivicOS at appropriate tier (per Companion 25 §2).
- Tier 2 functional coverage as community capacity supports.
- Civic Studio language pack contributions welcomed.
- Translation memory shared with community consent.
- Voice/IVR provisioning prioritized for languages whose speakers face exclusion otherwise.

### 7.3 Revitalization

- Where communities seek revitalization, platform provides resources.
- Language documentation under community control.
- Language pedagogy through CivicLearn integration if community chooses.
- AI multilingual capacity prioritizing equity.

### 7.4 Discipline

- Communities decide what to digitize, what to keep oral.
- No digitization without consent.
- Sacred or restricted speech protected per community protocols.

### 7.5 Forbidden

- Linguistic homogenization through platform design.
- Commercial use of indigenous language data.
- Extraction of language data without consent.

## 8. Customary law accommodation

### 8.1 The principle

Where compatible with constitutional rights, customary law and community processes are respected.

### 8.2 Mechanisms

- Customary dispute resolution recognized in CivicJustice (where statutorily authorized).
- Customary family structures supported in registrations.
- Customary land tenure supported in CivicLand.
- Customary inheritance respected within constitutional limits.

### 8.3 Tensions with constitutional rights

- Where customary practice and individual constitutional rights conflict, individual rights generally prevail.
- Vulnerable individuals (women, children, members in disagreement with community) protected against community pressure.
- Exit options preserved (e.g., a community member may opt for state-administered processes).

### 8.4 Bilateral evolution

- State-community frameworks evolve through dialogue.
- Communities have voice in how their customary law interacts with state law.
- Constitutional reviews include indigenous rights perspective.

## 9. Welfare, health, education in community contexts

### 9.1 Principles

- Programs designed with communities, not for them.
- Cultural appropriateness centered.
- Trauma-informed where historical state harms have occurred.
- Community workforce prioritized (community health workers, community educators).

### 9.2 Mechanisms

- CivicCare programs adapted to community contexts.
- CivicHealth integration with traditional medicine where communities wish.
- CivicLearn supports indigenous education systems and languages.
- Mobile services to remote communities with community-friendly approach.

### 9.3 Discipline

- Community partnership in design, delivery, evaluation.
- Community veto on capabilities affecting them.
- Community workforce trained and compensated equitably.
- Outcome metrics that respect community values.

### 9.4 Forbidden

- Programs imposed on communities without consent.
- Cultural bias in service delivery design.
- Use of welfare to coerce assimilation.
- Surveillance through service delivery.

## 10. Data sovereignty

### 10.1 The principle

Data about indigenous peoples and communities belongs to those communities.

### 10.2 Mechanisms

- Community-controlled data repositories.
- Community-determined access policies.
- Aggregate statistics about communities require community consent.
- Re-identification prohibitions enforced.

### 10.3 Civic Data Trusts adapted

- Indigenous data sovereignty principles applied.
- Community representation in data trust governance.
- Community veto on data uses.
- Benefit-sharing required where applicable.

### 10.4 Forbidden

- Commercial use of community data.
- Research on communities without community partnership.
- Surveillance through community data aggregation.
- Re-identification of community individuals.

## 11. Plural governance

### 11.1 The pattern

Indigenous self-government coexists with state government. CivicOS deployment respects this plurality.

### 11.2 Mechanisms

- Indigenous government infrastructure where communities operate it (own CivicOS deployments or alternatives).
- Inter-government protocols (state-indigenous) for cooperation.
- Mutual recognition of credentials where bilateral arrangements exist.
- Community representation in major platform governance decisions.

### 11.3 Discipline

- Indigenous governments have authority within their jurisdiction.
- State-indigenous coordination through bilateral protocols, not unilateral.
- Disputes resolved through mutually agreed processes.

### 11.4 Forbidden

- Use of platform to undermine indigenous self-government.
- Imposition of state governance frameworks where indigenous governance applies.
- Cooptation of indigenous leaders or processes.

---

## 12. Reparative posture

### 12.1 The principle

Where past digitization or state action has harmed indigenous communities, the platform's posture is reparative.

### 12.2 Mechanisms

- Acknowledgment of past harms.
- Repatriation of misappropriated data.
- Correction of historical records misrepresenting communities.
- Restitution where appropriate.
- Prevention of recurrence through structural safeguards.

### 12.3 Trust-building

- Multi-year engagement with patient relationship-building.
- Community-led pace and process.
- Community-chosen representatives.
- Independent verification of commitments.

### 12.4 Forbidden

- Performative apologies without substantive change.
- Reparative measures designed by state without community input.
- Continued patterns that produced harm.

---

## 13. Cross-border indigenous peoples

### 13.1 The pattern

Many indigenous peoples span state borders. CivicOS supports their continuity.

### 13.2 Mechanisms

- Cross-border identity recognition under planetary protocols (Companion 15 §6) for indigenous peoples spanning borders.
- Family reunification facilitation.
- Cross-border land tenure recognition where applicable.
- Cultural and linguistic continuity supported across sovereigns.

### 13.3 Discipline

- Sovereign autonomy preserved.
- Indigenous peoples' authority recognized regardless of state borders.
- No use of state borders to divide communities arbitrarily.

### 13.4 Forbidden

- Use of state borders to suppress indigenous community continuity.
- Commercial exploitation of cross-border indigenous data.

## 15. The standing question (community frame)

Annually, leadership asks publicly:

> "Have we engaged with indigenous peoples and self-determined communities on their terms? Has free, prior, and informed consent been respected? Has community sovereignty over identity, land, knowledge, language, and governance been honored? What needs to change?"

Answers come from communities themselves. Sovereign Trust Officer and Algorithmic Ombudsman incorporate community findings. Reparative measures continue.

---

## 16. Forbidden in indigenous and community engagement

CivicOS will not:

- Deploy without FPIC.
- Override community veto.
- Coopt community leaders or processes.
- Appropriate traditional knowledge.
- Exploit linguistic or cultural data.
- Facilitate dispossession.
- Surveil communities.
- Use platform to suppress self-determination.
- Operate cross-border in ways dividing communities.
- Continue harmful patterns after community objection.

This list grows; it does not shrink.

---

## 17. The community north star

A platform deployed in a society with indigenous peoples or self-determined communities is not deployed unless those peoples consent. A platform that serves them serves them on their terms, in their languages, with their leaders, on their lands, with their knowledge respected, with their sovereignty preserved.

The discipline is FPIC. The pace is community-determined. The relationship is bilateral. The posture is reparative where harms have occurred. The structural safeguards prevent recurrence.

When CivicOS becomes a tool of assimilation, dispossession, surveillance, or appropriation in indigenous or community contexts — even unintentionally, even efficiently — it has failed and must be reformed. Capability without community consent is colonial; capability with community consent is partnership.

The state that deploys CivicOS in a plural society has an obligation it cannot delegate: to engage with humility, to listen with patience, to act with consent, to remedy harm, to prevent recurrence, and to serve the diverse peoples who together constitute the polity.

This is not a special accommodation; it is fundamental practice for any state that takes self-determination seriously. CivicOS is built to support that seriousness — in design, in deployment, in operation, and in evolution.
