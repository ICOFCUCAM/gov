# CivicOS — Oceans, Submarine Cables, and Maritime Infrastructure (Companion 76)

This companion specifies how CivicOS approaches the oceans and undersea infrastructure that underwrites global digital communications and marine commons. It complements Companion 11 §5.2 (network infrastructure), Companion 15 §11 (planetary cooperation), Companion 52 (ecological commons), and Companion 75 (space) by going deep on a domain often invisible in DPI literature: the physical infrastructure beneath the seas.

The thesis: **submarine cables carry roughly 99% of intercontinental internet traffic; sovereignty in the digital age is partly underwater**. CivicOS recognizes the criticality of submarine cable infrastructure, ocean stewardship as commons, sovereign maritime infrastructure (where applicable), and indigenous and coastal community involvement in maritime governance. The infrastructure is invisible until it fails; the discipline is to think about it before that happens.

The discipline: submarine cable resilience through diversity; coastal landing site security; cross-sovereign cooperation on cable laying and maintenance; ocean ecological stewardship; coastal community involvement; sovereign maritime data; anti-weaponization of submarine infrastructure; indigenous coastal community sovereignty respected.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Submarine cables are critical infrastructure.** Recognized and protected.
2. **Ocean as commons.** Stewardship across sovereigns.
3. **Coastal community involvement.** Especially indigenous coastal peoples.
4. **Diversity and redundancy in cable routing.** Anti-single-point-of-failure.
5. **Sovereign cable landing security.** Physical and cyber.
6. **Cross-sovereign cooperation on cable infrastructure.** Treaty-based.
7. **Anti-weaponization of submarine infrastructure.** Cables are civilian.
8. **Marine biodiversity protected.** Cables routed and operated to minimize impact.
9. **Climate-adaptive maritime planning.** Sea level rise, storm intensity.
10. **Indigenous and customary maritime rights honored.** Where applicable.

---

## 2. Submarine cable infrastructure

### 2.1 The pattern

Submarine cables carry the vast majority of intercontinental internet traffic. Their routing, maintenance, and protection matter to digital sovereignty.

### 2.2 Mechanisms

- Multiple cable routes for redundancy.
- Cable diversity (different operators, different paths).
- Sovereign cable landing site security.
- Cooperation with cable maintenance operators.
- Cooperative funding for new cables in underserved regions.

### 2.3 Discipline

- Anti-single-point-of-failure in critical communications.
- Sovereign control of landing sites where strategically important.
- Multi-vendor for cable operators.
- Regular vulnerability assessment.

### 2.4 Forbidden

- Use of submarine cable monopoly to extract from sovereigns.
- Intentional damage to civilian submarine cables.
- Surveillance through submarine cable monopoly position.

---

## 3. Cable landing security

### 3.1 The principle

Coastal landing sites are physical points where submarine cables meet land — and points of vulnerability.

### 3.2 Mechanisms

- Physical security of landing sites.
- Cyber security at cable terminals.
- Sovereign oversight of landing operations.
- Anti-tampering monitoring.
- Cross-sovereign cooperation on landing security.

### 3.3 Discipline

- Anti-foreign-coercion of landing sites.
- Civilian operation with appropriate oversight.
- Multi-operator landing sites.

### 3.4 Forbidden

- Use of landing site control for surveillance of intercepted traffic beyond lawful intercept (Companion 24 §4).
- Use of landing site control to suppress communications.
- Foreign coercion compromising landing site sovereignty.

## 4. Ocean as commons

### 4.1 The principle

Beyond sovereign maritime zones, oceans are global commons. Beyond exclusive economic zones, common stewardship.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) compliance.
- Cross-sovereign cooperation on ocean governance.
- Marine protected areas.
- Sustainable fisheries.
- Anti-pollution.
- Climate ocean monitoring.

### 4.3 Discipline

- Sovereign authority within EEZ preserved.
- Common stewardship beyond.
- Anti-overfishing.
- Anti-pollution discipline.

### 4.4 Forbidden

- Pollution of international waters.
- Overfishing in international waters.
- Sovereign extension into international waters absent treaty.

## 5. Coastal communities

### 5.1 The principle

Coastal communities — fishing communities, port communities, indigenous coastal peoples — have specific interests in maritime governance.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

- Coastal community involvement in maritime planning.
- Sustainable fisheries support.
- Coastal cultural heritage protected.
- Coastal climate adaptation (sea level rise, storms).
- Indigenous coastal sovereignty (Companion 36).

### 5.3 Discipline

- FPIC for projects affecting indigenous coastal communities.
- Anti-displacement.
- Sustainable livelihoods supported.

### 5.4 Forbidden

- Coastal development displacing communities.
- Use of coastal infrastructure for surveillance of communities.
- Appropriation of indigenous coastal knowledge.

## 9. Anti-weaponization of submarine infrastructure

### 9.1 The principle

Submarine cables are civilian infrastructure. Their weaponization or use for offensive operations is forbidden.

### 9.2 Mechanisms

- International cooperation on cable protection.
- Anti-tampering monitoring.
- Treaty-based protection.
- Civilian operation only.

### 9.3 Discipline

- Anti-arms-race in undersea.
- Cross-sovereign cooperation on civilian protection.

### 9.4 Forbidden

- Use of submarine cables for offensive operations.
- Intentional damage to civilian cables.
- Weaponization of cable infrastructure.

## 14. The oceans north star

Oceans are humanity's largest commons. Submarine cables are civilization's hidden infrastructure. Coastal communities are guardians of much marine biodiversity. CivicOS supports responsible maritime governance through cable infrastructure resilience, ocean commons stewardship, coastal community involvement, marine biodiversity protection, climate-adaptive maritime planning, and anti-weaponization.

When CivicOS becomes complicit in submarine cable monopolization, surveillance through landing sites, coastal community displacement, marine biodiversity destruction, or maritime infrastructure weaponization — it has failed ocean stewardship. Capability without ocean discipline is not progress; it is the militarization of an essential commons.

When the platform supports responsible maritime infrastructure — through resilient cables, secure landing sites, coastal community partnership, marine biodiversity protection, climate adaptation, anti-weaponization, and indigenous community sovereignty — it earns the right to be infrastructure for civilizations that depend on the oceans.

The discipline is daily. The cable resilience is structural. The community involvement is real. The biodiversity protection is long-horizon. The civilian focus is preserved.

The oceans connect us. The submarine cables enable us. The coastal communities protect them. The platform exists to serve all three with respect, stewardship, and sovereignty — not extraction, surveillance, or weaponization.

This is the underwater infrastructure that makes everything else possible. Anything less abandons the commons that future generations and other species depend on.
