# CivicOS — Real-Time Civilization Simulation (Companion 151)

This companion specifies the evolution of CivicOS's public infrastructure digital twins (Companion 143) into a **real-time civilization simulation** — the integrated cross-domain modeling fabric that supports predictive national planning, AI-assisted policy testing, scenario governance, climate adaptation, and intergenerational stewardship. It complements Companion 143 (digital twins — per-domain), Companion 137 (sovereign cloud and AI compute — substrate), Companion 138 (constitutional AI — discipline), Companion 142 (NCCCs — convening), Companion 149 (NDNS — nervous system), Companion 150 (agent swarms — actors), and Volume II Parts 6–9 (post-2040 evolution).

The thesis: **a civilization simulation is not a god's-eye view of the polity; it is a structured model of common wealth and common risk, operated by the sovereign for public purpose, governed by constitutional discipline, accessible to civil society, and bounded so that it informs but does not replace democratic deliberation**. Simulations that purport to predict citizen behavior at individual resolution become surveillance models; simulations that purport to decide policy become technocratic substitutes for democracy; simulations that are vendor-controlled become foreign decision-making over sovereign substrate. CivicOS therefore builds civilization simulation as a public utility under constitutional discipline: aggregate by default; uncertainty disclosed; policy-testing-with-humans-deciding; sovereign-controlled; civil-society-accessible; intergenerationally stewarded; replaceable; honestly bounded.

The discipline: aggregate models not citizen-individual; uncertainty quantified and disclosed; policy testing surfaces options, humans decide; sovereign-controlled substrate per Companion 137 and 139; civil society standing access per Companion 74; constitutional officer oversight; AI Class C/D under charter per Companion 138; long-horizon stewardship per Future Generations Commissioner; cross-sovereign cooperation per Companion 140 and treaty; replaceability engineered; honest about what cannot be modeled.

## 3. Simulation scope (what is modeled)

### 3.1 Physical systems

- **Water**: reservoirs, rivers, aquifers, distribution, demand, quality.
- **Power**: generation, transmission, distribution, demand, storage, renewables.
- **Transport**: traffic, transit, freight, ports, airports, bridges.
- **Telecom**: capacity, congestion, routing.
- **Buildings**: stock, energy use, condition.
- **Land use**: zoning, change-over-time.
- **Marine and coastal**: sea level, currents, fisheries.

### 3.2 Ecological systems

- **Climate**: temperature, precipitation, extreme events.
- **Air quality**: pollutants, particulates.
- **Soil**: moisture, erosion, salinization.
- **Biodiversity**: species, habitat, indicators.
- **Forests and wetlands**: cover, health.

### 3.3 Human and economic systems

- **Population dynamics**: aggregate age structure, household formation, internal migration patterns.
- **Health**: aggregate disease burden, life expectancy, healthcare demand.
- **Education**: enrollment, attainment, demographic flows.
- **Labor and economy**: employment, wages, sectoral activity, prices.
- **Migration**: cross-border flows under treaty modeling.
- **Supply chains**: critical material flows; bottleneck modeling.

### 3.4 Public infrastructure stress

- **Disaster exposure**: flood, earthquake, cyclone, drought, wildfire, pandemic.
- **Critical infrastructure dependencies**: cross-system cascades.
- **Climate adaptation needs**: per region, per population.

### 3.5 What is NOT modeled at citizen-individual resolution

- Citizen behavior.
- Citizen movement (beyond aggregate flows).
- Citizen consumption.
- Citizen political views.
- Citizen social networks.
- Citizen relationships.

These are forbidden by architecture (per Companion 143 §6).

## 4. Aggregation and privacy

### 4.1 Floor of aggregation

Per Companion 143:

- Smallest published spatial unit statistically robust.
- Smallest published temporal unit statistically robust.
- Differential privacy applied to citizen-derived aggregates.
- k-anonymity / l-diversity where applicable.

### 4.2 Anti-individual-modeling

The simulation does not contain, derive, or infer citizen-individual states. Where individual-level data feeds aggregate computation (e.g., for population dynamics), the individual-level data is held in segregated systems and aggregated before entering the simulation.

### 4.3 Cross-domain privacy

Even where individual domains aggregate, cross-domain queries can re-identify (e.g., "single person of this age, in this neighborhood, with this medical condition"). The federation layer enforces cross-domain privacy through query-level privacy review.

### 4.4 Forbidden in aggregation

- Citizen-individual modeling.
- Cross-domain re-identification.
- Privacy floor bypassed by power user.
- Aggregation that masks per-protected-attribute disparities (different problem; required stratification).

## 5. Simulation engines

### 5.1 Physics-based

Where physics applies, physics-based models are preferred:

- Hydrological models (water flow, reservoir balance).
- Power flow models.
- Atmospheric and ocean models (climate, weather).
- Structural models (bridges, buildings under stress).
- Epidemiological models (compartmental SEIR-class).
- Traffic flow models.
- Crop and soil models.

### 5.2 Statistical and econometric

For systems where physics is incomplete:

- Demographic projections.
- Economic models (DSGE, agent-based macroeconomics, input-output, computable general equilibrium).
- Migration models.
- Education attainment models.

### 5.3 Agent-based

For systems with heterogeneous interacting actors (at aggregate level, not citizen-individual):

- Population-mobility patterns.
- Market dynamics with heterogeneous firms.
- Disease spread with heterogeneous communities.

### 5.4 AI surrogates

For fast approximation of expensive simulations:

- Per Companion 143 §8.2: AI surrogates Class C/D under charter.
- Validated against physics-based or empirical ground truth.
- Drift monitored.
- Replaceable by physics-based when surrogate fails.

### 5.5 Hybrid

Increasingly: physics + AI hybrid models (e.g., physics-informed neural networks; data-assimilation with physical priors).

### 5.6 Discipline

- Documented assumptions.
- Validation against real-world data.
- Uncertainty quantification.
- Anti-overfitting.
- Cross-validation.
- Open-source where feasible.

### 5.7 Forbidden in simulation engines

- AI surrogate replacing physics-based for high-stakes without validation.
- Hidden assumptions.
- Black-box vendor model for high-stakes policy use.
- Simulation outputs presented as certain.

## 6. Uncertainty quantification and disclosure

### 6.1 The principle

Every simulation output carries uncertainty. Disclosure is mandatory.

### 6.2 Mechanisms

- Confidence intervals per output.
- Sensitivity analysis per scenario.
- Validation gap disclosure (where model has been validated, where not).
- Ensemble methods (multiple models, range of outcomes).
- Tail risk quantification (not only median outcomes).

### 6.3 Plain-language uncertainty

Per Companion 22 and People's Editor:

- Citizens see uncertainty in plain language.
- Civil society can audit uncertainty methodology.
- Constitutional officers see uncertainty in their reviews.

### 6.4 Forbidden

- False certainty.
- Uncertainty hidden from policy-makers.
- Cherry-picking of confidence intervals.
- Use of uncertainty as cover for inaction or as cover for action.

## 7. Scenarios and policy testing

### 7.1 The pattern

Policymakers want to know: "if we do X, what happens?"

The simulation supports scenario testing:

- **Counterfactual**: "if drought worsens, what to expect."
- **Policy test**: "if we adopt this tax change, what economic effects."
- **Climate adaptation**: "if we build this seawall, how does flood risk change."
- **Disaster preparedness**: "if a magnitude-7 earthquake here, what response capacity needed."
- **Economic shock**: "if commodity X price doubles, what fiscal impact."
- **Demographic**: "with these immigration scenarios, what infrastructure needs."
- **Migration**: "with these climate scenarios, what migration patterns."

### 7.2 Citizen and civil society scenarios

- Citizens can submit scenarios for evaluation (per scope; not infinitely).
- Civil society can request scenarios.
- Citizens' Assembly per Companion 19 can request scenarios on deliberation topics.
- Results published with full methodology.

### 7.3 Anti-simulation-as-decision

The simulation tests scenarios; humans decide. The simulation:

- Surfaces options.
- Quantifies trade-offs.
- Discloses uncertainty.
- Does not commit policy.

The Class is firmly Class D (recommendation only) or Class E (sovereign coordination assistance) — never policy decision.

### 7.4 Forbidden in scenarios

- AI policy decisions via simulation.
- Citizen-individual scenario modeling.
- Suppression of unfavorable scenarios.
- Use of scenarios for political reward.

## 20. The real-time civilization simulation north star

A civilization simulation is not a god's-eye view; it is a structured model of common wealth and common risk operated by the sovereign for public purpose. CivicOS builds civilization simulation as public utility under constitutional discipline: aggregate by default; uncertainty disclosed; policy testing with humans deciding; sovereign-controlled substrate; civil-society-accessible; constitutional-officer-supervised; long-horizon-stewarded; replaceable; honestly bounded.

When CivicOS becomes infrastructure where simulation purports to predict citizen behavior at individual resolution, where uncertainty is hidden from policymakers, where policy is laundered through "what the model says," where adverse scenarios are suppressed, where vendor-locked models capture sovereign policy, where civil society is excluded, where constitutional officers are bypassed, where long-horizon thinking is collapsed into short-term electoral cycles, where citizen-individual risk scoring re-enters under "predictive societal risk" framing — it has failed at the simulation discipline. Capability without simulation-discipline is the institutionalization of technocratic substitution for democratic deliberation.

When the platform supports simulation that aggregates honestly, discloses uncertainty plainly, surfaces options without committing them, runs on sovereign-controlled substrate, opens to civil society audit, briefs constitutional officers, integrates Future Generations Commissioner concurrence on long-horizon, respects treaty boundaries cross-sovereign, and operates within the absolute prohibition on citizen-individual modeling — it earns the right to be infrastructure for plural democracies that face climate, demographic, economic, and disaster challenges with informed deliberation rather than ignorance or technocracy.

The discipline is daily. The aggregation is enforced. The uncertainty is disclosed. The decisions are human. The substrate is sovereign. The civil society is active. The officers are present. The horizons are intergenerational. The treaties bind. The prohibitions on individual modeling are absolute.

The simulation models common wealth and common risk. Democracy decides what to do about them. The platform's job is to keep that separation real — to give citizens, civil society, constitutional officers, and parliament a structured shared picture without collapsing the shared picture into the decision. Anything less builds technocratic substitution wearing simulation's costume — and a polity that lets simulation decide for it has not built a future, it has surrendered one.
