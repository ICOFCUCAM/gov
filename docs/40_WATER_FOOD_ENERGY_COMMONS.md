# CivicOS — Water, Food, and Energy Commons (Companion 40)

This companion specifies how CivicOS treats water, food, and energy — the trio of physical commons on which civilization rests. It complements Volume I §17 (CivicGrow), §21 (CivicBuild), §25 (CivicGreen) and the climate-coupled capabilities in Volume II by going deep on the operational discipline these commons require under conditions of growing scarcity, climate stress, and rising population.

The thesis: **water, food, and energy are not commodities like any other; they are commons whose continued availability is foundational to all other rights and capacities**. CivicOS treats them as critical infrastructure, with strong sovereign control over essentials, equitable access guarantees, climate-coupled planning, anti-corruption discipline, and cross-sovereign cooperation on shared resources.

The discipline: nobody dies of thirst, hunger, or cold for lack of access in a CivicOS jurisdiction. Markets operate, but with floors. Climate stress is anticipated, not lamented. Sovereign control of critical infrastructure preserved. Cross-sovereign cooperation on shared waters and grids is treaty-anchored.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Floors before markets.** Universal basic access guaranteed before market mechanisms operate.
2. **Climate-coupled planning.** Long-horizon planning integrates climate scenarios.
3. **Sovereign control of essentials.** Critical infrastructure remains under sovereign authority.
4. **Equitable access measured.** Disparities tracked and remediated.
5. **Anti-corruption discipline.** Resource allocation transparent and auditable.
6. **Conservation as priority.** Demand management alongside supply expansion.
7. **Cross-sovereign cooperation on shared resources.** Treaty-anchored; sovereignty preserved.
8. **Indigenous and community rights honored.** Customary water, food, land rights respected.
9. **Future generations' interests protected.** Intergenerational equity in resource decisions.
10. **Market signals where helpful, regulation where required.** Pragmatic mix.

## 2. Water

### 2.1 Universal access

- Right to safe drinking water as constitutional commitment.
- Water service infrastructure as critical infrastructure.
- Pricing structures that don't exclude (lifeline tariffs).
- Disconnection protections; alternative provision for vulnerable.

### 2.2 Infrastructure

- Sovereign or municipal water authority operates core infrastructure.
- CivicCity (Volume I §12) and CivicBuild (Volume I §21) integrate water systems.
- Real-time monitoring of pressure, quality, leaks, demand.
- Predictive maintenance integrated with infrastructure twin.
- Multi-source water supply (surface, groundwater, recycled, desalinated where applicable).

### 2.3 Watershed management

- Watershed-scale planning coupled with environment twin.
- Source water protection.
- Cross-watershed cooperation under treaty (rivers spanning sovereigns).
- Indigenous and community water rights respected.

### 2.4 Climate adaptation

- Drought planning per region.
- Flood resilience.
- Aquifer sustainability.
- Salinity intrusion management.
- Climate twin coupling for long-horizon planning.

### 2.5 Discipline

- Water quality monitoring continuous.
- Anti-corruption in water utility operations.
- Customary and indigenous water rights protected.
- Anti-privatization of essential water services without strict regulation.

### 2.6 Forbidden

- Disconnection of vulnerable populations from essential water.
- Privatization that compromises universal access.
- Use of water access as coercion.
- Watershed exploitation that compromises future capacity.

### 3.3 Distribution

- Anti-spoilage programs.

- Cooperative buying and bulk-sale infrastructure.

### 3.4 Access

- Affordable food prices through market function with safety nets.
- Food assistance through CivicCare for low-income households.
- School feeding for child nutrition.
- Maternal nutrition programs.
- Anti-malnutrition surveillance and intervention.

### 3.5 Utilization (nutrition)

- Nutrition education.
- Cultural appropriateness in nutrition guidance.
- Anti-stunting and anti-wasting programs.
- Food safety regulation.
- Anti-adulteration.

### 3.6 Climate adaptation

- Climate-resilient cropping.
- Drought, flood, locust early warning.
- Cross-sovereign food security cooperation under planetary protocols.
- Strategic reserves.

### 3.7 Discipline

- Anti-corruption in food assistance and procurement.
- Anti-discrimination in food access.
- Food sovereignty for indigenous communities.
- Anti-monopoly in food supply chains.

### 3.8 Forbidden

- Food access as coercion.
- Privatization of food security infrastructure that compromises access.
- Algorithmic food rationing without human oversight.

## 5. Cross-cutting commons discipline

### 5.1 Floors before markets

- Universal basic access guaranteed.

- Disconnection protections legal.

### 5.3 Climate-coupled planning

### 5.4 Anti-corruption

- Procurement transparency (OCDS).
- Beneficial ownership of suppliers.
- Anti-collusion AI in tenders.
- Whistleblower protections.

### 5.5 Equity measurement

- Stratified access reporting.
- Investment targeting underserved areas.
- Disparity remediation.

## 7. Indigenous and community rights

### 7.1 The principle

Indigenous peoples and communities have specific water, food, and land-based rights respected.

### 7.2 Mechanisms

- Customary water rights protected.
- Traditional food systems supported.
- Customary land tenure honored (Companion 36).
- Free, prior, and informed consent for projects affecting community resources.
- Benefit-sharing for resource use on community lands.

### 7.3 Discipline

- Community veto on extractive projects affecting community resources.
- Anti-displacement.
- Cultural continuity protection.

### 7.4 Forbidden

- Resource development on community lands without FPIC.
- Use of state resource policy to dispossess communities.
- Commercial exploitation without benefit sharing.

## 8. Future generations

### 8.1 The principle

Resource decisions must consider future generations' interests.

### 8.2 Mechanisms

- Future Generations Commissioner consulted on long-horizon resource decisions.
- Aquifer sustainability standards.
- Soil conservation requirements.
- Climate-coupled long-horizon planning.
- Capital investment with intergenerational returns prioritized.

### 8.3 Discipline

- Short-horizon resource extraction that exhausts future capacity rejected.
- Sustainability metrics integrated into NEOS (Companion 14).
- Public reporting on sustainability outcomes.

### 8.4 Forbidden

- Resource policies that exhaust future capacity for present consumption.
- Irreversible resource decisions without long-horizon assessment.
- Discounting of future generations' interests in optimization.

## 11. Forbidden

CivicOS will not:

- Permit disconnection of essential water, food, or energy from vulnerable populations.
- Allow privatization that compromises universal access.
- Use resource access as coercion.
- Permit algorithmic resource rationing without human oversight and equity safeguards.
- Allow resource policies that exhaust future capacity.
- Permit cross-border arrangements that compromise sovereign rights or commitments.
- Allow extractive projects on community lands without FPIC.
- Permit climate-incompatible long-horizon investments without strict justification.
- Allow surveillance through resource utility operations.

This list grows; it does not shrink.

## 13. The commons north star

Water, food, and energy are not commodities to be optimized; they are commons on which everything else depends. A society that provides them universally, equitably, sustainably, and resiliently has secured the foundation. A society that doesn't has failed at the most basic.

CivicOS treats these as constitutional commitments. Markets operate; floors hold. Climate stress is anticipated. Sovereign control of essentials preserved. Cross-sovereign cooperation on shared waters and grids is treaty-anchored. Indigenous and community rights respected. Future generations' interests protected.

When the platform permits disconnection from essentials, when privatization compromises access, when commons are extracted for present consumption at future expense, when communities are dispossessed of customary rights — it has failed the foundation. Capability without commons stewardship is not progress; it is the privatization of survival.

When the platform secures these commons for all, with dignity, equity, sustainability, and resilience — it earns the right to be civilization infrastructure. The discipline is daily. The protection is structural. The stewardship is intergenerational.

These are the commons. They belong to everyone. The platform exists to keep them so.
