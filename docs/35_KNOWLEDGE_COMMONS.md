# CivicOS — Public Knowledge Commons and Scientific Infrastructure (Companion 35)

This companion specifies how CivicOS supports the public knowledge commons — open science, public-funded research, scientific infrastructure, scholarly communication, evidence in policy, and the long-term preservation of knowledge. It complements Companions 13 §6 (Civic Data Trusts) and 23 (National Digital Twin) by going deep on the *knowledge layer* on which informed governance and informed citizenship depend.

The thesis: **knowledge is sovereign infrastructure**. A state that cannot reliably produce, access, share, evaluate, and preserve knowledge cannot govern well, cannot innovate, cannot adapt to change, cannot bequeath capability to the next generation. CivicOS treats public knowledge as a commons — open by default, sovereign-stewarded, accessible across borders for shared challenges, and protected from enclosure.

The discipline: publicly funded research outputs are open. Public scientific data is shared through Civic Data Trusts. Indigenous and community knowledge is protected and respected on community terms. Long-term preservation is a sovereign commitment. Knowledge does not get locked up in commercial paywalls or weaponized for surveillance.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Public funding produces public knowledge.** State-funded research outputs are open access.
2. **Knowledge is sovereign infrastructure.** Treated with the seriousness of identity or payments.
3. **Open by default.** Closed only with documented justification (privacy, security, narrow commercial).
4. **Long-term preservation is a sovereign duty.** Decades-to-centuries horizon.
5. **Indigenous and community knowledge respected.** Communities steward; platform supports.
6. **Free flow of scientific information across borders.** Within sovereign discretion on rights matters.
7. **Citizens have access.** Knowledge is not just for experts.
8. **Truth-telling discipline.** No suppression of inconvenient findings; no manipulation of data.
9. **Evidence in policy.** Decisions backed by best-available evidence; uncertainty honest.
10. **Pluralism of methods and interpretations.** Knowledge advances through diverse approaches.

## 3. Open access to publications

### 3.1 The principle

Publicly funded research outputs are openly accessible to the public who funded them.

### 3.2 Mechanisms

- Mandatory open-access deposit for state-funded research.
- Sovereign or consortium-operated open-access repositories.
- Embargo periods minimal (standardized; not vendor-controlled).
- Plain-language summaries for non-specialists.
- Multilingual abstracts where feasible.

### 3.3 Discipline

- Researchers retain copyright with open-license requirements.
- Article processing charges, where applicable, do not subsidize commercial paywalls.
- "Hybrid" open-access (paying twice) discouraged.
- Compliance monitored; non-compliance affects funding eligibility.

### 3.4 Forbidden

- Public funding of research locked behind commercial paywalls absent strict justification.
- Publishing arrangements that compromise research integrity.
- Embargo periods exceeding statutory limits without justification.

## 4. Open data

### 4.1 The principle

Research data underlying publications is shareable; broader public data is open by default.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- Data management plans required for funded research.
- Data deposited in trusted repositories.
- Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable (FAIR) standards.
- Civic Data Trusts (Companion 13 §6) as fiduciaries for sensitive datasets.
- Persistent identifiers (DOIs, ARKs) for citation.

### 4.3 Discipline

- Privacy floors apply (differential privacy, k-anonymity for human-subject data).
- Indigenous knowledge protected per community protocols.
- Sensitive ecological data (endangered species locations, etc.) protected.
- Commercial use limited per Civic Data Trust governance.

### 4.4 Forbidden

- Selling public data for commercial extraction.
- Re-identification attempts on anonymized public data.
- Use of public data for surveillance.

## 5. Open educational resources

### 5.1 The principle

Learning materials freely accessible to enable lifelong learning across populations.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

- Sovereign-curated OER libraries.
- Multilingual coverage for national languages.
- Accessibility-compliant formats.
- Civic Studio support for community contributions.
- Integration with CivicLearn for credentialing.

### 5.3 Discipline

- Educational content reviewed for accuracy, accessibility, cultural appropriateness.
- Anti-discrimination in materials.
- Plurality of perspectives in pluralistic societies.
- Indigenous and minority cultural content represented.

### 5.4 Forbidden

- Commercial replacement of public education through OER capture.
- Politicized content censoring legitimate intellectual diversity.
- Algorithmic curation that narrows learning.

## 6. Open source and open hardware

### 6.1 The principle

Public-funded code and hardware designs are open source where feasible.

### 6.2 Mechanisms

- Sovereign open-source repositories.
- Open hardware design repositories.
- Permissive licensing for foundational code.
- Source-available with sovereign carve-outs for productized modules (Companion 19 §5).
- Civic Studio support.

### 6.3 Discipline

- Security review before publication.
- Export control compliance.
- Sustainable maintenance commitments.
- Contribution licensing clear.

### 6.4 Forbidden

- Public funding of code locked privately.
- Open source-washing (claiming open while restricting).
- Open hardware that enables prohibited capabilities (e.g., autonomous weapons, mass surveillance).

## 7. Long-term preservation

### 7.1 The principle

Knowledge belongs to future generations as much as to current. The state has a duty to preserve.

### 7.2 Horizons

- 10 years: routine archival.
- 50 years: deliberate preservation; format migration.
- 100 years: extraordinary preservation; redundant copies; multiple media.
- 500+ years: civilizational preservation; deep-time considerations.

### 7.3 Mechanisms

- National archives integration.
- Multi-format preservation (born-digital + emulation + migration).
- Multi-site replication.
- Cryptographic integrity (hash-chained).
- Periodic restoration tests.
- Cross-sovereign cooperation on civilizational archives.

### 7.4 Discipline

- Selection criteria transparent and contestable.
- Privacy floors apply (some materials sealed for statutory periods).
- Cultural representation across communities.
- Indigenous community say in what is preserved and how.

### 7.5 Forbidden

- Erasure of historical record for political convenience.
- Selective preservation favoring dominant culture.
- Commercial-only preservation that locks heritage behind paywalls.

## 8. Indigenous and community knowledge

### 8.1 The principle

Indigenous and community knowledge is sovereign to the communities. The platform supports their stewardship; the platform does not appropriate or extract.

### 8.2 Mechanisms

- Communities determine what to share, with whom, on what terms.
- CARE principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) for indigenous data governance.
- Protocols for traditional knowledge respected.
- Benefit-sharing agreements where commercial use occurs.
- Repatriation of misappropriated knowledge.

### 8.3 Discipline

- Community consent per use.
- Cultural appropriateness review.
- Recognition of community sovereignty.
- Anti-extraction discipline.

### 8.4 Forbidden

- Commercial appropriation without community consent.
- Use of traditional knowledge for purposes communities have not authorized.
- Erosion of community sovereignty over their knowledge.

## 9. Scientific infrastructure

### 9.1 The pattern

Science needs infrastructure: labs, observatories, sensors, supercomputers, large-scale collaborations. CivicOS supports sovereign and multilateral scientific infrastructure.

### 9.2 Sovereign scientific infrastructure

- National research institutes funded sustainably.
- National laboratories (where applicable to sovereign capability).
- Observation networks (climate, environment, health).
- Supercomputing for science (sovereign or cooperative).

### 9.3 Multilateral scientific infrastructure

- Cross-sovereign cooperation under treaty (e.g., CERN-class projects, observation satellites, genomic projects, climate science).
- Civic Data Trust governance for shared data.
- Reciprocity principles.

### 9.4 Discipline

- Research integrity standards enforced.
- Conflict of interest disclosed.
- Replication encouraged.
- Pre-registration for hypothesis-driven research.
- Open peer review where feasible.

### 9.5 Forbidden

- Suppression of inconvenient findings.
- Manipulation of data.
- Scientific fraud unaddressed.
- Use of scientific infrastructure for surveillance or weapons absent narrow legitimate authority.

## 10. Public statistics

### 10.1 The pattern

CivicStat (Volume I §38) operates the national statistical system. The integrity of statistics is sovereign critical.

### 10.2 Discipline

- Statistical office independence anchored constitutionally where possible.
- Methodology transparent and stable.
- Definitions stable; revisions public.
- Disaggregated data published; equity visible.
- Continuous statistics by 2040 (Companion 09 — CivicStat).

### 10.3 Multidimensional metrics

By 2045: GDP supplemented by multidimensional flourishing metrics (Companion 14 §8).

### 10.4 Forbidden

- Political manipulation of statistics.
- Suppression of inconvenient indicators.
- Methodology changes timed for political purposes without proper review.
- Commercial use of micro-data without strict governance.

## 11. Evidence in policy

### 11.1 The principle

Decisions are backed by best-available evidence; uncertainty is acknowledged.

### 11.2 Mechanisms

- Independent research advisory bodies for major policy areas.
- Systematic reviews and rapid evidence assessments.
- Pre-passage simulation of major policies (Vol II §3.5).
- Distributional impact assessments (Companion 14 §5).
- Long-horizon impact assessments (Companion 13 §3.4).

### 11.3 Discipline

- Evidence does not replace politics; it informs.
- Multiple methods, multiple interpretations honored.
- Uncertainty quantified and communicated.
- Civil society and academic critique welcomed.

### 11.4 Forbidden

- Cherry-picking evidence for political purposes.
- Commissioning research to support pre-decided positions.
- Suppression of evidence inconvenient to decisions.
- Use of evidence to delegitimize political alternatives.

## 12. Truth-telling discipline

### 12.1 The principle

The state tells truth to citizens. About state performance, about challenges, about uncertainty, about mistakes.

### 12.2 Mechanisms

- Public dashboards updated near-real-time.
- Plain-language explanations.
- Honest uncertainty communication.
- Post-incident reports public.
- Independent fact-checking infrastructure encouraged.

### 12.3 Discipline

- Information vacuums avoided (rumors fill them).
- Contradictory messages between agencies addressed quickly.
- Officials accountable for false statements.
- Press freedom protected.

### 12.4 Forbidden

- State lies to citizens.
- Suppression of journalism reporting on state.
- AI-generated impersonation content from state.
- Differential information delivery based on political alignment.

## 13. Cross-sovereign knowledge cooperation

### 13.1 Mechanisms

- Cross-sovereign Civic Data Trusts under planetary protocols (Companion 15 §13).
- Open access reciprocity.
- Joint scientific infrastructure (climate, health, space, ocean, particle physics).
- Sabbatical and exchange programs.
- Cross-sovereign archives cooperation.

### 13.2 Discipline

- Sovereign authority preserved over what to contribute.
- Sovereign-sensitive knowledge remains sovereign.
- Citizens may opt out of contributions of their data.
- Equitable access — knowledge commons benefit all participants, not just contributors.

### 13.3 Forbidden

- Knowledge cooperation that compromises sovereign rights commitments.
- Extraction from low-resource sovereigns to high-resource sovereigns without reciprocity.
- Use of shared knowledge infrastructure for surveillance or coercion.

## 15. Anti-disinformation

### 15.1 The pattern

Information ecosystems face disinformation challenges. CivicOS supports truth-telling without becoming a censor.

### 15.2 Mechanisms

- Cryptographic signing of state communications (impersonation impossible).
- Provenance metadata for media (image/video signing).
- Source attribution standards.
- Independent fact-checking infrastructure supported.
- Media literacy in education.

### 15.3 Discipline

- Citizens decide what to believe; the state does not.
- No platform-level censorship of lawful speech.
- Press freedom absolute.
- Civil society standing in information ecosystem governance.

### 15.4 Forbidden

- Use of "anti-disinformation" as cover for political censorship.
- Algorithmic determination of "true" or "false" speech in civic surfaces.
- State-generated synthetic political content.
- Use of disinformation infrastructure to surveil journalists or civil society.

## 17. The knowledge north star

A state that controls knowledge controls everything. A state that frees knowledge enables everything. CivicOS commits to the second path: public knowledge as commons, scientific infrastructure as sovereign capability, indigenous knowledge as community sovereignty, long-term preservation as duty to future generations, evidence in policy as discipline, truth-telling as default.

When knowledge is enclosed for commercial advantage, the commons has been raided. When knowledge is suppressed for political convenience, the citizen has been disrespected. When knowledge is appropriated from communities, sovereignty has been violated. When knowledge is weaponized for surveillance, the platform has failed.

The discipline is daily. The openness is structural. The preservation is intergenerational. The respect for community sovereignty is non-negotiable. The truth-telling is honest.

When CivicOS becomes a tool of knowledge enclosure, manipulation, or suppression — even unintentionally, even with good intent — it has failed and must be reformed. Capability without epistemic discipline is danger; capability with epistemic discipline is the foundation of self-governing societies.

This is how civilization preserves and extends what it learns, generation by generation, sovereignty by sovereignty, community by community, citizen by citizen.
