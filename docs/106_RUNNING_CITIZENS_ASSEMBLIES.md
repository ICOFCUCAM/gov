# CivicOS — Running Citizens' Assemblies Operationally (Companion 106)

This companion provides operational detail on running Citizens' Assemblies — the sortition-based deliberative bodies referenced throughout the corpus. It complements Companion 13 §5 (Citizens' Assembly establishment), Companion 74 (civil society engagement), Companion 22 (dignity and rights), and Companion 44 (implementation cookbook) by being specifically operational: how does one actually run an assembly so it serves citizens rather than legitimizes pre-decided outcomes.

The thesis: **a poorly-run Citizens' Assembly is worse than no assembly**. Performative assemblies that legitimize predetermined outcomes corrode trust. Genuine assemblies build civic capacity and produce substantive recommendations. The difference is in operational discipline: sortition rigor, balanced information, independent facilitation, adequate time, fair compensation, genuine response to recommendations.

The discipline: sortition stratified and auditable; balanced information packets; independent facilitation; adequate time and compensation; cultural and linguistic accommodation; trauma-informed where applicable; genuine state response within statutory window; outcomes documented and tracked.

## 2. When to convene

Citizens' Assemblies are appropriate for:

- Substantive policy questions requiring deliberation.
- Contested matters where political process is deadlocked.
- Long-horizon decisions where political incentives favor short-horizon.
- Algorithmic governance questions affecting citizens broadly.
- Constitutional or quasi-constitutional matters.
- Indigenous community-affecting platform changes (per Companion 36, with FPIC).
- Major capability deployments.
- Era reviews (per Companion 41).

Citizens' Assemblies are not appropriate for:

- Routine operational decisions.
- Decisions where citizen input is performative.
- Matters where deliberation would harm vulnerable populations.
- Quasi-judicial individual cases.

## 3. Sortition mechanics

### 3.1 Selection pool

Random selection stratified for:
- Geographic distribution.
- Demographic representation (age, gender, ethnicity, disability where applicable).
- Socioeconomic background.
- Education level (mixed; not just degree-holders).
- Indigenous community representation per population share or invitation.
- Urban/rural balance.
- Language community representation.

### 3.2 Process

- Public sortition algorithm (auditable).
- Independent stratification verification.
- Invitations sent to selected.
- Replacement selection for declines.
- Documentation of selection process.

### 3.3 Size

Typical 100-500 participants:
- 100-150 for focused single-question assemblies.
- 200-500 for major civic capacity reviews or era reviews.
- Smaller for community-specific (within community per Companion 36).

### 3.4 Discipline

- Anti-algorithmic-discrimination in selection.
- Anti-political-filtering.
- Transparent process.

### 3.5 Forbidden

- Hand-picked participants.
- Selection biased toward specific demographics absent legitimate basis.
- Algorithmic sortition that systematically excludes.

## 4. Compensation

### 4.1 The principle

Participation requires time citizens may not have without compensation. Fair compensation enables broader participation.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- Lost wage compensation.
- Per diem for time.
- Travel costs.
- Childcare during sessions.
- Accessibility support costs.
- Lost benefits replaced (welfare beneficiaries don't lose benefits for participating).

### 4.3 Discipline

- Anti-tokenism (small honorariums that exclude low-income).
- Anti-pressure to accept inadequate compensation.
- Cultural sensitivity in compensation framing.

### 4.4 Forbidden

- Compensation that excludes vulnerable populations.
- Compensation used to coerce specific positions.
- Discrimination in compensation.

---

## 5. Information packets

### 5.1 The principle

Participants need balanced, accessible, substantive information to deliberate well.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

- Independent commissioning of information.
- Multiple expert perspectives represented.
- Plain language (reviewed by People's Editor).
- Multilingual.
- Accessible formats per Companion 46.
- Visual aids where useful.
- Pre-reading time built in.

### 5.3 Discipline

- Anti-one-sided-framing.
- Anti-information-overload.
- Cultural appropriateness.

### 5.4 Forbidden

- One-sided information packets.
- Pre-loaded conclusions.
- Information withheld that affects deliberation.

---

## 6. Expert input

### 6.1 The principle

Experts inform deliberation; they don't replace it.

### 6.2 Mechanisms

- Multiple experts representing diverse perspectives.
- Affected community representatives included.
- Civil society representatives.
- Academic representatives.
- Question-and-answer time substantial.
- Anti-expert-monopoly on framing.

### 6.3 Discipline

- Anti-expert-capture of deliberation.
- Participants empowered to question.
- Plurality respected.

### 6.4 Forbidden

- Expert-only deliberation with citizens as audience.
- Expert framing that predetermines conclusions.
- Suppression of contested expert perspectives.

---

## 7. Facilitation

### 7.1 The principle

Independent facilitation enables genuine deliberation.

### 7.2 Mechanisms

- Trained facilitators independent of state and contested parties.
- Diverse facilitators (cultural, linguistic, demographic).
- Trauma-informed facilitation where applicable.
- Anti-bias training.
- Equal participation supported.

### 7.3 Discipline

- Anti-direction by facilitators.
- Plurality of voices supported.
- Quiet participants encouraged.

### 7.4 Forbidden

- Facilitators directing toward predetermined conclusions.
- Discrimination among participants by facilitators.
- Suppression of unpopular but lawful views.

---

## 8. Deliberation process

### 8.1 The pattern

Multi-week process typically:
- Initial orientation.
- Information sessions with experts.
- Small group deliberation.
- Plenary discussion.
- Iterative refinement.
- Final recommendations.
- Voting on recommendations.

### 8.2 Discipline

- Adequate time (typically 3-8 weekends or equivalent).
- Anti-rushing.
- Iterative refinement supported.

### 8.3 Forbidden

- Deliberation forced into predetermined outcomes.
- Time pressure designed to prevent substantive deliberation.
- Suppression of dissent within deliberation.

## 9. Cultural and linguistic accommodation

Per Companion 25:

### 9.1 Mechanisms

- Multilingual sessions.
- Cultural traditions of deliberation respected where applicable.
- Indigenous community processes supported where chosen (per Companion 36).
- Religious accommodations.
- Disability accommodations per Companion 46.

### 9.2 Discipline

- Anti-imposition of dominant culture deliberation style.
- Anti-discrimination in participation.

### 9.3 Forbidden

- Discrimination in deliberation.
- Cultural insensitivity.
- Suppression of cultural deliberation styles within constitutional bounds.

## 10. Recommendations and voting

### 10.1 Mechanisms

- Structured recommendation development.
- Final voting on recommendations.
- Minority report capability.
- Documentation of dissent.
- Anonymized voting where appropriate.

### 10.2 Discipline

- Anti-consensus-coercion.
- Plurality preserved.
- Minority views honored.

### 10.3 Forbidden

- Coerced consensus.
- Suppression of minority views.
- Manipulated voting.

---

## 11. State response

### 11.1 The principle

State must respond to recommendations within statutory window — accepting, modifying, or rejecting with rationale.

### 11.2 Mechanisms

- Statutory response window (typically 90-180 days).
- Detailed response to each recommendation.
- Implementation tracking.
- Public reporting on implementation.

### 11.3 Discipline

- Anti-token-response.
- Honest engagement with recommendations.
- Specific rationale for rejection.

### 11.4 Forbidden

- Non-response to recommendations.
- Token acceptance without implementation.
- Politicized rejection without rationale.

---

## 12. Continuous accountability

### 12.1 Mechanisms

- Implementation tracking by independent body.
- Civil society monitoring.
- Participant follow-up engagement.
- Annual reporting on assembly outcomes.

### 12.2 Discipline

- Anti-cover-up of implementation failures.
- Honest reporting.

### 12.3 Forbidden

- Suppression of implementation tracking.
- False implementation claims.
- Use of implementation gaps for political purposes.

---

## 13. Participant safety

### 13.1 The principle

Assembly participation should not expose participants to harm.

### 13.2 Mechanisms

- Confidentiality of personal participation details.
- Anti-retaliation for participation.
- Trauma-informed for sensitive matters.
- Mental health support available.
- Safe spaces during deliberation.

### 13.3 Discipline

- Anti-targeting of participants by state, employer, or community.
- Anti-doxxing of participants.

### 13.4 Forbidden

- Retaliation against participants for their views.
- Surveillance of participants beyond legitimate purposes.
- Use of participation data for adverse decisions.

## 14. Cross-sovereign assembly cooperation

Per Companion 15:

### 14.1 The principle

Cross-sovereign assemblies may engage shared challenges (climate, AI safety, planetary commons).

### 14.2 Mechanisms

- Multi-sovereign sortition.
- Cross-cultural facilitation.
- Multi-language deliberation.
- Sovereign opt-outs honored.

### 14.3 Discipline

- Sovereign authority preserved.
- Anti-cooptation by single sovereign.
- Equitable participation.

### 14.4 Forbidden

- Cross-sovereign assemblies compromising sovereign authority.
- Coerced cross-sovereign participation.

## 17. The Citizens' Assembly operational north star

A Citizens' Assembly done well builds civic capacity, surfaces public deliberation, produces substantive recommendations, and earns trust. Done badly, it legitimizes pre-decided outcomes, corrodes trust, and wastes participants' time.

The discipline is operational: sortition rigorous and auditable, balanced information, independent facilitation, adequate time, fair compensation, cultural and linguistic accommodation, trauma-informed where applicable, genuine state response, outcomes documented.

When Citizens' Assemblies become performative theater for pre-decided outcomes, when participants are tokenized, when facilitators direct, when state responses are token, when implementation is hollow — assemblies have failed at their purpose. Capability without deliberative discipline is not progress; it is the institutionalization of legitimization theater.

When assemblies operate with discipline, plurality, genuine deliberation, and substantive state response — they earn the right to be infrastructure for democratic societies that take deliberation seriously.

The discipline is daily. The sortition is rigorous. The deliberation is genuine. The state response is honest. The implementation is tracked.

Citizens' Assemblies are one mechanism for democratic engagement. They don't replace voting, civil society, journalism, or other democratic infrastructure. They supplement them, surfacing voices that might otherwise be unheard, building capacity citizens might otherwise not develop.

Anything less abandons participants to performative engagement that corrodes the trust the platform depends on.
