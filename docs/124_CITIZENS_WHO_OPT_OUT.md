# CivicOS — Citizens Who Opt Out of Digital Engagement (Companion 124)

This companion specifies how CivicOS serves citizens who choose to engage minimally or not at all with digital infrastructure — through preference, philosophy, religious conviction, cultural practice, or trauma history. It complements Companion 22 (dignity and rights), Companion 12 (interaction evolution including inclusion floor), Companion 46 (disability accommodation), Companion 36 (indigenous communities), Companion 51 (religious affairs), and Companion 87 (LGBTQ+ in restrictive contexts) by being specifically about citizens who opt out.

The thesis: **the inclusion floor includes the right to not use digital infrastructure**. Some citizens won't, can't, or shouldn't engage digitally. They remain full citizens entitled to full service. CivicOS's commitments only matter if they hold for those who reject digital engagement as much as for those who embrace it.

The discipline: opt-out as right; in-person and paper paths preserved equivalently; no penalty for opting out; no data accumulation for opt-out citizens; no algorithmic decisions about opt-outs; cultural and religious accommodation; trauma-informed for those whose opt-out comes from harm; long-horizon preservation of non-digital paths.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Opt-out is right.** Not exception requiring justification.
2. **In-person and paper paths preserved equivalently.** No second-class.
3. **No penalty for opting out.** Service equal regardless.
4. **No data accumulation for opt-outs.** Minimal records.
5. **No algorithmic decisions about opt-outs.** Human officers only.
6. **Cultural and religious accommodation.** Sincere beliefs respected.
7. **Trauma-informed for those whose opt-out comes from harm.**
8. **Long-horizon preservation of non-digital paths.** Not phased out.
9. **Heightened privacy for opt-outs.** They want it.
10. **Respectful engagement.** Without proselytizing digital.

## 2. Why citizens opt out

### 2.1 Philosophical

- Privacy concerns.
- Anti-surveillance principles.
- Skepticism of state digital infrastructure.
- Preference for face-to-face relationship with state.
- Concerns about algorithmic governance.

### 2.2 Religious

- Some religious traditions limit digital engagement.
- Sabbath and observance considerations.
- Concerns about data in religious matters.

### 2.3 Cultural

- Some cultural traditions favor in-person interaction.
- Indigenous community traditions per Companion 36.
- Customary practices of local engagement.

### 2.4 Trauma-related

- Survivors of state surveillance.
- Refugees from regimes that used digital infrastructure against citizens.
- Victims of identity theft.
- Trauma survivors with privacy heightened concerns.

### 2.5 Practical

- Lack of digital access.
- Lack of digital literacy at preferred level.
- Preference for paper records.

### 2.6 Discipline

- Anti-judgment of opt-out reasons.
- Reasons not demanded.
- Respectful accommodation.

### 2.7 Forbidden

- Demanding opt-out justification.
- Discriminating based on opt-out reasons.
- Surveillance of opt-out citizens through their non-engagement.

## 17. The opt-out citizens north star

The inclusion floor includes the right to not use digital infrastructure. CivicOS commits to serving opt-out citizens with full dignity, equivalent service quality, in-person and paper paths preserved, no penalty for opting out, no algorithmic decisions about them, cultural and religious accommodation, trauma-informed engagement, and long-horizon preservation of non-digital paths.

When CivicOS becomes a tool of digital-pushing, stigmatizing opt-outs, discrimination through algorithmic decisions on minimal data, or phasing out non-digital paths for cost savings — it has failed the inclusion floor that should be inviolable. Capability without opt-out respect is not progress; it is the institutionalization of digital-only citizenship.

When the platform serves opt-outs with the same dignity, the same service quality, the same democratic engagement as digital citizens — it earns the right to be infrastructure for pluralistic societies that include citizens with diverse engagement preferences.

The discipline is daily. The non-digital paths are preserved. The cultural and religious accommodations are real. The trauma-informed engagement is structural. The long-horizon commitment is genuine.

Opt-out citizens are full citizens. Their decision to engage minimally with digital infrastructure does not diminish their citizenship. The platform's commitments must hold for them as much as for those who engage digitally. Anything less abandons citizens to digital-only futures they never consented to.
