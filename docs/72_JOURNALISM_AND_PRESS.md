# CivicOS — Journalism, Press Freedom, and Public Information (Companion 72)

This companion specifies how CivicOS supports journalism, press freedom, and citizens' access to public information. It complements Companion 24 §11 (civil society protection), Companion 35 §12 (truth-telling), Companion 53 §3 (public broadcasting), and Companion 29 (citizens' field guide) by going deep on the operational and ethical design of platform infrastructure that supports a functioning press without surveilling, controlling, or substituting for it.

The thesis: **journalism is essential to democratic accountability, and a state platform that surveils, controls, or competes with journalism has misunderstood its role**. CivicOS supports journalism through structural press freedom protection, source confidentiality, FOI infrastructure, public information access, anti-impersonation safeguards, and structural anti-state-propaganda commitments. Journalism is independent civil society; the platform serves citizens' right to information through journalists, not despite them.

The discipline: press freedom absolute; source protection structural; FOI infrastructure functional; public information genuinely public; state communications signed and verifiable but never AI-impersonating; no platform-driven content moderation; civil society and independent journalism standing access to platform data; anti-retaliation against journalists strict; state-funded journalism (where applicable) editorially independent.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Press freedom is constitutional.** Not platform-grant; structural commitment.
2. **Source confidentiality protected.** Including from state and platform.
3. **FOI infrastructure functional.** Not just nominal.
4. **Public information genuinely public.** Anti-data-hoarding.
5. **State communications signed.** Anti-impersonation.
6. **No state-generated synthetic political content.** Even from public broadcasting.
7. **No algorithmic content moderation in civic surfaces.** Citizens decide what to read.
8. **Anti-retaliation against journalists strict.** Across digital and physical.
9. **State-funded journalism editorially independent.** Insulation from political pressure.
10. **Civil society and journalism standing access to platform.** For accountability journalism.

## 2. Press freedom infrastructure

### 2.1 Structural protections

- Constitutional press freedom anchored.
- Anti-prior-restraint.
- Anti-criminal-defamation (where constitutionally appropriate).
- Anti-SLAPP (strategic lawsuits against public participation) protections.
- Journalist registration not required (anyone can be a journalist).

### 2.2 Source protection

- Source protection privilege recognized.
- Cryptographically anonymous source channels (per Companion 21 §13).
- Anti-subpoena of journalist sources.
- Anti-surveillance of journalist communications.

### 2.3 Anti-retaliation

- Civil and criminal protections against retaliation for journalism.
- Algorithmic targeting of journalists forbidden.
- Surveillance of journalists in lawful work forbidden.
- Cross-border data sharing exposing journalists refused.

### 2.4 Discipline

- Press freedom monitored continuously.
- Attacks on press documented.
- Constitutional officers (Algorithmic Ombudsman, Sovereign Trust Officer) investigate.

### 2.5 Forbidden

- Surveillance of journalists in lawful work.
- Algorithmic targeting of journalists.
- Use of platform to suppress press.
- Cross-border data sharing exposing journalists to retaliation.
- Criminal defamation prosecutions (where constitutionally inappropriate).

## 3. Source protection

### 3.1 The principle

Journalism depends on protected sources. Without source protection, much accountability journalism is impossible.

### 3.2 Mechanisms

- Onion-routed submission channels.
- No metadata logging of submitter contacts.
- Two-key encryption (submission to journalist; reply through onion-routed channels).
- Legal privilege for journalist source relationships.
- Anti-subpoena protections.

### 3.3 Discipline

- Confidentiality strict.
- Anti-pretextual investigation.
- Constitutional officer protection if pressured.

### 3.4 Forbidden

- State subpoena of journalist source identity absent extraordinary circumstances.
- Surveillance to identify journalist sources.
- Use of platform to facilitate identification.

---

## 4. FOI infrastructure

### 4.1 The principle

Citizens have right to information held by state. FOI mechanisms make right real.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- Online FOI request portal.
- Multilingual access.
- Time-bounded response.
- Independent appeals.
- Public log of requests and responses (anonymized).
- Plain-language denials with rationale.

### 4.3 Discipline

- Anti-pretextual denial.
- Narrow interpretation of exemptions.
- Anti-discrimination in FOI processing.

### 4.4 Public information

- Open data by default for non-sensitive aggregates.
- Structured access to public dashboards.
- Civil society and journalism standing access where appropriate.

### 4.5 Forbidden

- Pretextual denial of FOI requests.
- Charging fees that exclude.
- Bureaucratic delay as denial.
- Algorithmic FOI processing absent human judgment.
- Discrimination in FOI processing.

## 5. State communications

### 5.1 The principle

State communications must be signed, verifiable, and honest. Anti-impersonation is structural.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

- All state communications cryptographically signed.
- Provenance metadata mandatory.
- Verifiable in Civic Wallet.
- Anti-impersonation alerts where forged communications detected.
- Multilingual.
- Plain language reviewed by People's Editor.

### 5.3 Discipline

- Honesty in state communications.
- Anti-misleading-framing.
- Cultural appropriateness.
- Anti-political-instrumentalization of state communications.

### 5.4 Forbidden

- Unsigned state communications.
- AI-generated political content from state.
- AI-impersonating-officials communications.
- State-generated synthetic content for political purposes.
- Personalized state communications based on political profiling.

## 6. Public broadcasting

Per Companion 53 §3:

### 6.1 The principle

Public broadcasting (where it exists) serves cultural, educational, and civic functions distinct from commercial media.

### 6.2 Mechanisms

- Editorial independence anchored statutorily or constitutionally.
- Public funding insulated from political pressure.
- Multilingual programming.
- Multicultural representation.
- Educational and civic content.
- Local and regional programming.
- Plurality of voices.

### 6.3 Discipline

- Editorial independence absolute.
- Anti-political-appointment to editorial positions.
- Anti-funding-cuts as political punishment.
- Anti-commercial-encroachment.

### 6.4 Forbidden

- Use of public broadcasting for state propaganda.
- Political control of public broadcasting editorial.
- Funding cuts as political punishment.
- AI-generated political content from public broadcasting.

## 7. Anti-disinformation

Per Companion 35 §15:

### 7.1 The principle

Information ecosystems face disinformation challenges. CivicOS supports truth-telling without becoming a censor.

### 7.2 Mechanisms

- Cryptographic signing of state communications.
- Provenance metadata for media.
- Source attribution standards.
- Independent fact-checking infrastructure supported.
- Media literacy in education through CivicLearn.

### 7.3 Discipline

- Citizens decide what to believe.
- No platform-level censorship of lawful speech.
- Press freedom absolute.
- Civil society standing in information governance.

### 7.4 Forbidden

- Use of "anti-disinformation" as cover for political censorship.
- Algorithmic determination of "true" or "false" speech in civic surfaces.
- State-generated synthetic political content.
- Use of disinformation infrastructure to surveil journalists or civil society.

## 12. Internet shutdowns

### 12.1 The principle

Internet shutdowns affecting platform services and broader information access are harmful and contested.

### 12.2 CivicOS posture

- CivicOS strongly opposes internet shutdowns affecting platform services.
- Where shutdowns occur, fallback channels (USSD, SMS, IVR) preserved.
- Civil society standing to challenge shutdowns.
- Constitutional officers monitor shutdown impacts.

### 12.3 Discipline

- Sovereign authority over telecommunications respected within constitutional limits.
- Public reporting on shutdowns and impacts.
- Anti-suppression-of-information through technical means.

### 12.4 Forbidden

- Use of platform to facilitate internet shutdowns.
- Targeted shutdowns of journalist or civil society communications.
- Shutdowns during elections to suppress information.

## 16. The journalism north star

Journalism is essential to democratic accountability. A state platform that surveils, controls, or competes with journalism has misunderstood its role. CivicOS supports journalism through structural press freedom protection, source confidentiality, FOI infrastructure, public information access, anti-impersonation safeguards, and structural commitments against state propaganda.

When CivicOS becomes a tool of journalist surveillance, source identification, FOI suppression, state propaganda generation, or algorithmic content moderation in civic surfaces — it has failed democratic accountability. Capability without press freedom is not progress; it is the institutionalization of information control.

When the platform serves journalism — through press freedom protection, source confidentiality, FOI infrastructure, signed state communications, no synthetic political content, civil society and academic access, anti-retaliation enforcement — it earns the right to be infrastructure for democratic societies that depend on independent journalism.

The discipline is daily. The press freedom is structural. The source protection is real. The FOI is functional. The civil society standing is honored.

Journalism does the work of democratic accountability. The platform supports the work; it does not surveil it, control it, or replace it. The citizen's right to information is realized through independent journalism, not through state-controlled information channels.

This is what makes a state platform worthy of democracy: support for the institutions that hold the state accountable, including the journalists who investigate the state itself.
