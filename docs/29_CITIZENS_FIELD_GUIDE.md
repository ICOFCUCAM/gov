# CivicOS — The Citizen's Field Guide (Companion 29)

This companion is written for citizens — for the person standing in line, the elder reaching for a paper certificate, the parent applying for school placement, the small-business owner filing a return, the inspector reporting a safety hazard, the displaced person seeking a provisional credential, the journalist asking for budget data. It describes what they can expect from CivicOS, what they have a right to, what they should look out for, and how they can push back.

The thesis: **the platform is the citizen's, not the state's**. Every architectural choice in CivicOS exists to make the citizen the principal of their own civic life. This document is the citizen-facing summary of those commitments — written in plain language, organized by what citizens actually need.

If a citizen ever experiences something that contradicts this guide, that is not a feature; that is a failure, and they have ways to push back. This guide tells them how.

---

## 1. What CivicOS owes you

### 1.1 Identity that belongs to you

- An identity you can use across services — once enrolled, it works everywhere.
- A wallet on your phone (or USSD on a feature phone, or at an agent in person) that holds your credentials, your money, your consents, your documents.
- The right to enroll even if you have no smartphone, no fixed address, no formal documents. There is always a path.
- Your biometric data stored only as encrypted templates that the state cannot reconstruct or share without your consent.
- A different pseudonymous identifier for each service — services cannot easily link you across purposes without your permission.

### 1.2 Money that flows when it should

- Benefits you're entitled to arrive on time.
- Tax filings pre-filled where the state already has the information.
- Payments cleared in seconds.
- Misdirected payments rolled back within 24 hours.
- Receipts cryptographically signed — you can prove what you paid and what you received.
- Wallet alternatives in cash through agents, with the same dignity.

### 1.3 Services that respect you

- Plain language in every interaction.
- Your language, your modality (voice, text, visual, in-person).
- Reasonable wait times; honest progress updates.
- Decisions explained in language you can understand.
- Appeal paths visible and time-bound.
- A human officer available for any consequential matter.

### 1.4 Privacy that is structural

- The state does not surveil your lawful private life.
- Your data does not flow between agencies without your consent (with narrow exceptions for crime, public health emergencies, etc., all judicially gated).
- You can see who has accessed your data, when, why.
- You can correct errors in your record.
- Sensitive interactions (medical, legal, social services) carry the strictest privacy regime.

### 1.5 Voice that matters

- Easy reporting of problems.
- Structured deliberation forums on policies that affect you.
- Citizens' Assemblies for major civic choices.
- Visible follow-through on your input.
- Whistleblower protection if you report wrongdoing.

### 1.6 Recourse that works

- Every consequential decision is appealable.
- Appeals time-bound; results explained.
- Legal aid available; representation supported.
- Independent constitutional officers (Algorithmic Ombudsman, People's Editor, Inspector General, Future Generations Commissioner) you can write to.
- Courts as ultimate recourse; standing for citizens to enforce rights.

### 1.7 Truth-telling

- When the state makes a mistake about you, it acknowledges, apologizes, and remedies.
- It does not blame you for its error.
- It does not punish you for reporting the error.
- It learns and changes.

## 2. What you can do with the wallet

### 2.1 Daily

- Show your ID at a service counter.
- Pay government fees.
- Receive benefits.
- Apply for a permit.
- File a tax return.
- Sign a document.
- Receive notifications from the state — every one of them signed and verifiable.

### 2.2 Periodic

- Renew a license.
- Register a child for school.
- Update an address.
- Confirm eligibility for a benefit.
- Vote (where the wallet integrates with electoral commission infrastructure).

### 2.3 Lifetime

- Birth registration of your child.
- School enrollment.
- Marriage registration.
- Property registration.
- Business registration.
- Pension claim at retirement.
- Will and estate registration.

### 2.4 Protection

- Manage who can see what about you (consents).
- Revoke access at any time.
- See who has accessed your data.
- Export your data and move to another wallet implementation if you wish.

### 2.5 Voice

- Submit feedback on services.
- Participate in deliberation forums.
- Contact constitutional officers if needed.

## 3. What the wallet should never do

- Show you ads.
- Optimize for your engagement.
- Track you between services without consent.
- Surveil your conversations or location.
- Be inaccessible without your password or biometric.
- Be opaque about what it has done in your name.
- Lock you out of essential services without due process.
- Send you unsigned messages from the "state" (those are scams).

If you ever experience any of these, report to the Wallet Authority and the Algorithmic Ombudsman.

## 4. What if you don't have a smartphone

CivicOS does not require a smartphone. The inclusion floor is real:

- **USSD twin** (`*civic#` on most networks): essential services on any feature phone — payment, balance, simple permits, emergency.
- **IVR** in your language: voice-driven service interaction.
- **SMS** for asynchronous notifications and confirmations.
- **Agent network**: in-person service with biometric authentication for cash-out, payment, identity confirmation.
- **Walk-in offices**: empowered officers in every district; mobile units for remote areas.
- **Paper alternative**: where digital is impossible, paper still works.

A citizen on USSD must get the same outcome as a citizen on a smartphone. If they don't, that is a failure to be reported.

## 5. What if you have a disability

The platform must work for you:

- WCAG 2.2 AA (or current standard) compliance audited per release.
- Voice and screen reader compatibility.
- Sign language video for major announcements.
- Tactile sign for deafblind citizens.
- Large-text and high-contrast modes.
- Easy-read versions of major content.
- Accommodations available without bureaucratic friction.
- Support for assistive technology.

If accessibility fails, report to the Algorithmic Ombudsman and the Inclusion Minister.

## 6. What if you're in a vulnerable situation

Children, elders, people with disabilities, refugees, people without homes, victims of violence, recently bereaved — the platform offers heightened care:

- Trained officers with appropriate training (gender-sensitive, trauma-informed, language-appropriate).
- Companion presence permitted.
- Document waivers where appropriate.
- Time extensions on deadlines.
- Stricter privacy protections on your situation.
- Use of vulnerability information for discriminatory purposes is forbidden.

## 7. What if AI made a decision about you

You have rights:

- **The right to know**: the decision and its basis.
- **The right to a rationale**: in language you can understand.
- **The right to a human review**: any AI-touched consequential decision can be reviewed by a human officer.
- **The right to appeal**: through documented channels.
- **The right to reversibility**: Class C+ decisions can be reversed within a defined window.
- **The right to reasonable assistance**: legal aid, plain language, support to navigate the appeal.

If you suspect bias or discrimination, contact the Algorithmic Ombudsman.

---

## 8. What if the state makes a mistake about you

The state should:

- Acknowledge promptly.
- Apologize unambiguously.
- Remedy: full restoration plus compensation for downstream harm.
- Learn: post-incident review; service improvement.
- Protect: citizens who experienced state error are not subsequently penalized.

If the state does not do these, escalate:

1. Direct supervisor of the officer involved.
2. Domain ombudsperson.
3. Algorithmic Ombudsman (if the error involved AI).
4. Inspector General (if officer misconduct is suspected).
5. People's Editor (if communication is the issue).
6. Courts.

---

## 9. What if you experience corruption

You have:

- **Whistleblower channels**: cryptographically anonymous reporting (web, mobile, USSD, in-person at independent offices, accredited civil society intermediaries).
- **Legal protection**: anti-retaliation enforced; representation supported.
- **Material support**: where appropriate, including relocation if needed.
- **Recourse to constitutional officers**: Inspector General, Algorithmic Ombudsman, Anti-Corruption Commission, Auditor General.
- **Public information**: open dashboards, OCDS, beneficial ownership, all available for your scrutiny.

---

## 10. What if you want to participate in policy

Multiple paths:

- **Citizens' Assemblies**: stratified random selection; if invited, your participation is compensated and weighty.
- **Public consultations**: formal comment periods on proposals.
- **Petition mechanisms**: if you and others can gather statutory thresholds, the matter receives parliamentary attention.
- **Standing civil society councils**: if you're affiliated with civil society organizations, structured access to deliberations.
- **Direct contact with constitutional officers**.
- **Voting**: in elections, run by independent electoral commission on dedicated infrastructure.

## 11. What about your data

### 11.1 You can

- See what data the state holds about you.
- See who has accessed it, when, why.
- Correct errors.
- Export it.
- Limit access through consent management.
- Move to a different wallet implementation.
- Refuse opt-in data uses.

### 11.2 The state can

- Hold the data necessary to provide services.
- Share data between agencies under specific consent or under narrow lawful authorities.
- Use anonymized aggregates for statistics, research, and policy.
- Use data for anti-fraud (with safeguards) and anti-corruption.
- Use data for public health emergencies (with safeguards).

### 11.3 The state cannot

- Surveil your lawful private life.
- Track you across services without consent.
- Share your data with foreign governments without local court order.
- Use your data for commercial purposes.
- Sell your data.
- Profile you for political opposition.
- Build "threat" classifications of you based on lawful activity.

If the state crosses these lines, escalate.

## 12. What about your children

The state owes them:

- Care for their best interests as primary consideration.
- Highest protection of their data.
- Privacy from commercial exploitation, absolute.
- Educational access protected.
- Health access protected.
- Protection from violence and exploitation.
- Voice in matters affecting them, age-appropriately.
- No algorithmic decisions affecting them without enhanced human review.

## 13. What about your elderly relatives

The platform should serve them with:

- Tactile, large-text, voice-first interfaces by default.
- Trusted-person delegation if they choose.
- Anti-fraud protections elevated.
- Walk-in service preserved.
- Patient support; unhurried interactions.
- Their language and dialect.

---

## 14. What about end of life and bereavement

- Death registration triggers automatic suspension of obligations.
- Estate processes streamlined.
- Pension and benefit transitions automated where lawful.
- Bereaved family supported, not burdened with paperwork.
- Inheritance workflows respect customary practices within constitutional limits.

If your bereaved family is asked to repeatedly prove a death or pursue obligations the deceased no longer owes, that is a failure. Escalate.

---

## 15. What about religion, culture, language

- Religious holidays observed in service availability.
- Multiple calendar systems supported.
- Cultural concepts respected (naming, kinship, customary practice).
- Your language at parity with all other national languages.
- Indigenous and minority language stewardship belongs to your community.

If you experience cultural disrespect, report to the People's Editor.

---

## 16. What about emergencies

- The state should warn you in your language, on every channel you use.
- Notifications signed and verifiable; impersonation impossible.
- Emergency services dispatched fast; followed up afterward.
- Anticipatory action where possible (the state should reduce risk before you experience it).
- Even in emergencies, mass surveillance is forbidden.
- Emergency powers expire automatically.

---

## 17. What you should be cautious of

### 17.1 Impersonation

If you receive a "state" message that is not signed and verifiable in your wallet, it is a scam. Report it.

### 17.2 Pressure

If an officer pressures you to provide information beyond what's necessary, or to pay something undocumented, that is misconduct. Report it.

### 17.3 Surveillance creep

If you see capabilities being added that look like surveillance, raise concerns publicly. Civil society and constitutional officers have channels for this.

### 17.4 Dark patterns

If a service uses pressure tactics, manipulative defaults, or asymmetric opt-in/opt-out, that is forbidden. Report to the People's Editor.

### 17.5 Discrimination

If you experience differential treatment based on protected characteristics, report to the Algorithmic Ombudsman and the relevant ministry.

### 17.6 Loss of voice

If your reports go unheard, your appeals fail without explanation, your recourse is blocked — escalate, including to civil society, journalism, and ultimately to courts.

## 18. Citizens' rights as the state evolves

As CivicOS grows from 2030 dashboards to 2050+ ambient civic computing, your rights do not diminish. They strengthen.

- **2030**: faster services, signed notifications, basic appeal rights.
- **2035**: proactive personal civic agent under your delegation; deliberation forums; right to disconnect.
- **2040**: ambient civic environment; right to a human guaranteed; voice-first service in your language.
- **2045**: spatial deliberative environments; continuous reskilling entitlement; long-horizon impact considered in decisions affecting you.
- **2050+**: presence-based civic computing; planetary mutual recognition; sovereignty for sovereigns and citizens preserved.

Across all eras: you are the principal. The platform serves you. AI is never your principal.

## 20. What you can ask the platform

You can ask:

- "What does the state know about me?"
- "Who has accessed my data, when, why?"
- "What entitlements am I eligible for?"
- "What's the status of my application?"
- "How do I appeal this decision?"
- "How do I report this problem?"
- "How is my region doing on (health / education / environment / etc.)?"
- "How was this decision made?" (rationale)
- "Who can I talk to about this?"

You cannot use the platform to ask about other citizens (except where legitimate purpose, such as parent-child relationships or court-authorized processes, applies).

## 22. The standing question (citizen frame)

Each year you can ask publicly:

> "Has CivicOS served me and my community? Has it respected my dignity, protected my privacy, listened to my voice, given me real recourse? What needs to change?"

The state owes you an honest answer. Civil society will help shape the answer. Constitutional officers will report. Courts will adjudicate. You can vote and you can speak.

The platform is yours. The state serves you. Your dignity is non-negotiable. Your privacy is structural. Your voice matters. Your appeal is real. Your time is respected. Your language is at parity. Your culture is honored. Your children are protected. Your elders are served. Your community has voice. Your future generations have a champion.

Where any of this is not true in practice, the platform has failed you and the platform must change. There are people, processes, and institutions designed to ensure that change. Use them.

You are the principal. Always.
