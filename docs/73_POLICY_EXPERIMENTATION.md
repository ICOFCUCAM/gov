# CivicOS — Policy Experimentation and Public Sector Innovation Labs (Companion 73)

This companion specifies how CivicOS supports policy experimentation, evaluation, and public sector innovation labs. It complements Companion 56 (sovereign R&D), Companion 14 (NEOS), Companion 23 (national digital twin), and Companion 13 (institutional re-engineering) by going deep on the operational and ethical design of evidence-based policy iteration.

The thesis: **good policy emerges from honest experimentation, evaluation, and iteration, not from declaration**. CivicOS supports policy experimentation through randomized pilots where ethically appropriate, rigorous evaluation, transparent reporting of failures alongside successes, citizen and civil society participation in experiment design, and protection against using "experimentation" as cover for harmful interventions on vulnerable populations.

The discipline: ethics first; informed consent for experimental subjects; vulnerable populations protected; control groups not denied benefits absent ethical justification; results published honestly; failures preserved as learning; civil society and citizen participation real; experiments do not become permanent without legitimate decision-making.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **Ethics first.** Policy experimentation under research ethics frameworks.
2. **Informed consent.** For experimental subjects where applicable.
3. **Vulnerable populations protected.** Anti-extraction.
4. **Control groups treated ethically.** Not denied benefits absent justification.
5. **Honest evaluation.** Results published; failures preserved.
6. **Citizen and civil society participation.** In experiment design.
7. **Algorithmic Ombudsman scrutiny.** Of experiments affecting citizens.
8. **Anti-permanent-pilot.** Experiments transition or end.
9. **Cross-sovereign learning.** Sharing of evidence under appropriate frameworks.
10. **Indigenous and community sovereignty.** FPIC for experiments affecting communities.

---

## 2. Public sector innovation labs

### 2.1 The pattern

Many sovereigns establish "innovation labs" or equivalent within government to test new approaches to service delivery, policy, and administration.

### 2.2 Mechanisms

- Innovation lab establishment within government.
- Cross-departmental cooperation.
- Civil society and academic partnerships.
- Time-bounded missions.
- Transparent evaluation.

### 2.3 Discipline

- Ethical oversight required.
- Anti-capture by narrow interests.
- Anti-extraction from public service capacity.
- Honest reporting.

### 2.4 Forbidden

- Innovation labs as cover for political reward.
- Capture of innovation funding by privileged interests.
- Use of innovation labs to circumvent constitutional commitments.

---

## 3. Experimental policy design

### 3.1 The pattern

Some policies can be tested in pilots before broader rollout. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental designs, and structured pilots can inform policy.

### 3.2 Mechanisms

- Ethics review for experimental policy designs.
- Informed consent where individuals affected differently.
- Stratified design ensuring vulnerable populations not disadvantaged.
- Transparent registration of experimental protocols.
- Pre-registered evaluation criteria.
- Independent evaluation.

### 3.3 Discipline

- Anti-experimentation-on-vulnerable-without-consent.
- Anti-control-group-denial-of-benefits.
- Equity in experiment design.
- Cultural appropriateness.

### 3.4 Forbidden

- Experimental policy interventions on vulnerable populations without informed consent.
- Use of "experimentation" to deny benefits to control groups when intervention is known beneficial.
- Algorithmic experiments on citizens without disclosure.
- Commercial experimentation on citizen data.

---

## 4. Algorithmic experimentation

### 4.1 The principle

Algorithmic systems are sometimes A/B tested. CivicOS limits this for civic surfaces.

### 4.2 Mechanisms

- A/B testing forbidden for civic surfaces (per Companion 12 §11.1).
- Internal optimization (e.g., load balancing) acceptable.
- User research with informed consent.
- Citizen panels for design feedback.

### 4.3 Discipline

- Constitutional limits on persuasive design.
- Algorithmic Ombudsman scrutiny.
- People's Editor review for citizen-facing changes.

### 4.4 Forbidden

- A/B testing citizens for engagement on civic surfaces.
- Algorithmic experimentation on rights-affecting decisions.
- Hidden experiments on citizen experience.

---

## 5. Sandbox environments

### 5.1 The pattern

Regulatory sandboxes allow controlled experimentation with novel approaches under regulator supervision.

### 5.2 Mechanisms

- Sandbox application and admission.
- Time-bounded participation.
- Limited scope (geographic, demographic, sectoral).
- Heightened reporting.
- Citizen consent for sandbox participation.
- Exit criteria.

### 5.3 Discipline

- Anti-permanent-sandbox.
- Equity in sandbox access.
- Anti-regulatory-capture through sandboxes.

### 5.4 Forbidden

- Sandbox as cover for permanent regulatory exemption.
- Discrimination in sandbox access.
- Use of sandbox data for purposes beyond agreed.

Per Companion 14 §3 (NEOS):

### 6.1 Mechanisms

- Continuous evaluation of major programs.
- Counterfactual analysis.
- Distributional impact assessment.
- Long-horizon impact assessment.
- Outcome monitoring.

### 6.2 Discipline

- Honest reporting.
- Independent evaluation where consequential.

### 6.3 Forbidden

## 7. Civil society participation

### 7.1 The principle

Civil society and academic researchers participate in experiment design, conduct, and evaluation.

### 7.2 Mechanisms

- Civil society consultation on major experiments.
- Academic partnership.
- Standing access for accredited researchers (per Companion 35 and Companion 13 §6).
- Citizen participation in experiment design.
- Citizens' Assembly review where appropriate.

### 7.3 Discipline

- Anti-cooptation.
- Plurality of perspectives.
- Anti-discrimination in participation.

### 7.4 Forbidden

- Experiments on citizens without civil society or academic involvement where appropriate.
- Suppression of critical analysis.

---

## 8. Indigenous and community participation

### 8.1 The principle

Experiments affecting indigenous or self-determined communities require FPIC (Companion 36 §3).

### 8.2 Mechanisms

- Community-led experiment design where affecting communities.
- FPIC required.
- Community-stewarded evaluation.
- Cultural appropriateness.

### 8.3 Discipline

- Anti-experimentation-on-communities.
- Communities determine acceptable risk.
- Reparative posture for past extractive research.

### 8.4 Forbidden

- Experiments affecting indigenous or community members without FPIC.
- Extraction from community participants.
- Imposition of dominant culture experimental frameworks.

## 9. Cross-sovereign experimentation

### 9.1 Mechanisms

- Cross-sovereign learning under planetary protocols.
- Comparative policy analysis.
- Joint experiments under treaty.

### 9.2 Discipline

- Sovereign authority preserved.
- Equitable participation.
- Anti-extraction.

### 9.3 Forbidden

## 10. Failure preservation

### 10.1 The principle

Failed experiments are valuable learning. Preservation honest.

### 10.2 Mechanisms

- Failures documented and published.
- Learning integrated into future design.
- Failure narratives preserved (per Companion 26).
- Cross-sovereign sharing of failures.

### 10.3 Discipline

- Anti-erasure of failures.
- Anti-blame culture.
- Honest accounting.

### 10.4 Forbidden

- Suppression of failure narratives.
- Punishment of innovators for honest failure.

---

## 11. Forbidden in policy experimentation

CivicOS will not:

- Permit experimental policy interventions on vulnerable populations without informed consent.
- Allow use of "experimentation" to deny benefits to control groups when intervention is known beneficial.
- Permit algorithmic experiments on citizens without disclosure.
- Allow commercial experimentation on citizen data.
- Permit A/B testing citizens for engagement on civic surfaces.
- Allow algorithmic experimentation on rights-affecting decisions.
- Permit hidden experiments on citizen experience.
- Allow sandbox as cover for permanent regulatory exemption.
- Permit suppression of inconvenient evaluation findings.
- Allow politicization of evaluation.
- Permit experiments affecting indigenous or community members without FPIC.
- Allow extraction from community participants.
- Permit innovation labs as cover for political reward.

This list grows; it does not shrink.

## 13. The policy experimentation north star

Good policy emerges from honest experimentation, evaluation, and iteration. CivicOS supports this through ethical experimental design, informed consent, vulnerable population protection, civil society participation, indigenous community FPIC, honest evaluation including failure preservation, and cross-sovereign learning.

When CivicOS becomes a tool of unethical experimentation on citizens, suppression of inconvenient findings, extractive research on communities, or algorithmic manipulation of civic experience — it has failed evidence-based policy. Capability without ethical experimentation discipline is not progress; it is the institutionalization of policy by manipulation.

When the platform supports honest policy iteration — through ethics, consent, equity, civil society participation, community sovereignty, honest evaluation, failure preservation — it earns the right to be infrastructure for democratic societies pursuing better policies through legitimate means.

The discipline is daily. The ethics is structural. The participation is real. The honesty is required.

Policy is contested terrain; experimentation is one tool for resolving disagreement through evidence. The platform supports the tool with appropriate ethical guardrails, not as substitute for political deliberation but as input to it.
